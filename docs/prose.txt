==============================================================================
Forte grammar — commentary
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The language is Forte; its files end in ".ft"; the compiler is "fortec". The
productions live in docs/grammar.bnf, which holds nothing but rules. This file
holds everything else: the notation, the reasoning behind the rules, the
lexer's separator insertion, and the open questions. Section numbers here are
what the cross-references inside this file mean; the rules are named, so a
mention of <foo> is found in grammar.bnf by searching for it.
==============================================================================


==============================================================================
0. Notation
==============================================================================

Pure BNF:

    <name>      nonterminal         ::=     definition
    NAME        terminal            |       alternation
    "..."       terminal            '...'   terminal
    <empty>     the empty string

Angle brackets mark a nonterminal and mark nothing else, so everything
without them is a terminal: a spelling in quotes, or a token kind in
capitals. The quotes are not part of the terminal — "fn" is the two letters —
and the two kinds of quote mean the same thing, '...' being there for the
terminal that holds a '"' of its own.

BNF has no optional, repetition or grouping brackets, so every one of those
is written out as its own rule: an optional X becomes a nonterminal with an
<empty> alternative, and a repetition becomes a left-recursive list. The
convention here is that <x_opt> is "X or nothing", <x_list> is "one or more
X separated by commas" unless its rule says otherwise, and <x_seq> is a bare
juxtaposed sequence. <empty> derives nothing.

BNF also has no comment syntax, which is why this file exists: grammar.bnf is
all productions, and every line of commentary about them is here.

The terminals in capitals — IDENTIFIER, INT_LITERAL and the rest — are the
token kinds the lexer produces. A quoted terminal is one spelling and a
capitalised one is a class of them, which is the whole of the difference: a
rule can ask for "fn" or for any IDENTIFIER, and never for a particular one.
Section 6 gives the characters behind each, and there the same names are
nonterminals — the start symbols of a second, character-level grammar. The
parser sees them as atoms. EOF is the end of the input.

This grammar describes the *token* stream, not the character stream. Two
passes run before it:

  1. The preprocessor (src/prep/comments.rs) blanks comments to spaces,
     preserving line and column.
  2. The lexer (src/lex/lexer.rs) produces tokens and *inserts semicolons*
     at line breaks that end a statement, and nothing at all before a "}"
     (see section 7).

Only the ";" is ever synthesised, and only in a statement body. A "," is
always written: the entries of a struct, an enum, a match and of every
literal — a struct's, a map's, a set's — are separated by hand, and a newline
among them means nothing. A written ";" and an inserted one are the same
token to the parser, and the grammar does not distinguish them. Newlines are
otherwise invisible.


==============================================================================
1. Compilation unit
==============================================================================

A file is a module and the suite is every file compiled together, which are
the only two units there are: a module holds items, a <namespace_decl> nests
another inside the one it is written in (section 2), and the suite is the
root all of them hang from. Nothing above the suite is nameable, there being
nothing above it to name.

<item>, <visibility>. A declaration is private to its module until "pub"
exports it. "priv" is therefore only the default written down: it is accepted
wherever "pub" is — a file-scope item, a struct field, an impl member — and
changes nothing there. It exists to be said out loud, not to do anything.

"pub(suite)" is the third answer and the middle one: exported to the rest of
the suite and no further. It is what a helper wants when two modules share it
and nothing outside is meant to have it, and it is the only place a
visibility takes an argument — "suite" is what stands between a module and
the world, and there is no fourth unit to put in the parentheses. The "("
is glued to the "pub" as an attribute's is to its name: a space there ends
the visibility at the word and leaves the parenthesis for the parser to
complain about.

<declaration>. A <var_decl> at file scope is a global. A <const_decl> is the
compile-time constant, and stands there and in a block alike (section 2).

<import_decl>. An import names what it reaches and nothing more, so what it
reaches may be a tree: a group holds names under a common path, a group may
hold another, and a glob takes whatever the path holds.

    import shapes::circle;
    import shapes::circle as circ;
    import shapes::{circle, square::*, poly::{tri, quad}};
    import shapes::*;

The alias belongs to the leaf it renames and not to the import, which is what
lets one group hold a renamed name beside a plain one. A glob renames
nothing, having named nothing to rename: "a::* as b" says nothing and is
refused. What the tree is written as is spelling and stops at the parser —
what the compiler holds is one leaf for each name, with the path in front of
it already written on.

A path may start where it is written or somewhere else, and three words say
which: "suite" is the root of the suite, "super" the module above this one,
and "self" this one. "super" repeats, so "super::super::a" climbs twice. They
are the roots of any path and not an import's alone: "suite::limits::MAX"
reads the same in an expression, a type or a pattern (section 5).

An import is a declaration like any other, which is what makes "pub import"
the way to say a re-export: the names it brought in leave again under the
visibility written on it. That also puts it where a declaration stands rather
than at the top of a file only, so one may be written inside a block, near
whatever wanted it.

Five names arrive without an import, and they are exactly the ones the
language's own syntax builds. A literal is syntax for a type a library
declares — "0..10" builds a "Range", "{1: 2}" a "Map", "{1, 2}" a "Set", with
"#" making either of the last two the hashed kind — so the syntax is the
language's and the type is not. Requiring the import made "for i in 0..10"
refuse to compile in a file that had imported nothing, which is the most
ordinary loop there is turned down for a reason about libraries.

So "Range", "Map", "HashMap", "Set" and "HashSet" are looked for and bound
without being asked for, and nothing else is: the prelude is what the
literals name, which is a rule rather than a list somebody keeps adding to.
Each lives in the module named after it, lowercased — "Range" in "range",
"HashMap" in "hashmap" — so a suite that writes one of those modules gets the
literal working with nothing said here.

Two things keep it from being a cost. A prelude name loses to everything
written: a declaration of the file's own wins, and so does an import written
by hand, the same way round a glob already loses. And a module is read only
where the syntax that needs it is written, because a module read is a module
compiled — its fns are ordinary fns — and a program that writes no literal
should not carry five libraries it cannot call. A suite with no library at
all is left exactly as it was: the prelude adds no requirement, it spares an
import where the type is there to be found.

<attribute>. One "%" per attribute — "%inline", "%repr(C)",
"%derive(Show, Clone)" — and a list of them is a list, written one after
another with nothing between:

    %inline
    %cold
    pub fn panic(m: str): null

The sigil and the name are one token, so nothing separates them and no space
may: what the parser is handed is the name, and the "%" is spent before it
gets there. That character spells the remainder operator as well, and where
it stands is the whole of what tells the two apart — an operand in front of
it makes it the operator, and nothing in front makes it an attribute. It is
the rule "*" already follows, "a * b" being a product and "*b" a reference.

An attribute is a prefix of the declaration it annotates and not a statement
of its own, so the lexer inserts no separator at the end of one however many
lines the list runs to. The arguments' "(" is glued to the name, as the "["
of the older "#[...]" spelling was; a space there ends the attribute at the
name and leaves the parenthesis for the parser to complain about.

That leaves "#" with one use: "#{" makes a map or set literal hashed
(section 5). "@" has one too, and it is a macro (below).

The set of them is closed: the compiler knows every attribute there is, and
one it does not know is an error where it is written rather than something
ignored. No library defines one, and adding one is a change to the compiler —
which is what buys a misspelling an error naming what was meant. These are
the six:

  %symbol("malloc")     Gives a fn the exact symbol name it is compiled to,
                        in place of the mangled one. A name is otherwise
                        mangled as "__F" and then each part written as its
                        length and then its characters — "foo" in the
                        namespace "namespaces", of an i32 and a "mytype", is
                        "__F10namespaces3foo3i326mytype" — so that two
                        functions may share a name. The length is
                        what makes it unambiguous, and it is why nothing has
                        to be escaped on the way in: an "_" inside a name is
                        a character like any other, where a separator between
                        the parts would have had to reserve one. Nothing
                        outside the language can predict the result, so this
                        is what a call out to C, or a call in from it, is
                        written with. Takes one STRING_LITERAL.
  %must_use             Says the value a fn returns may not be thrown away:
                        an <expr_stmt> that discards it is an error. The word
                        is not "dropped", which section 2 spends on releasing
                        a value that owns something -- a thrown-away value is
                        released too, and the complaint is that nobody read
                        it first. Worth
                        having because "null" belongs to every type
                        (section 3), so a result nobody looked at is exactly
                        what a missed failure looks like. "let _ = f()" is
                        how to say the value really was meant to go.
  %inline               A hint, and the one thing here a backend may ignore.
  %noinline             The other direction, and the more useful of the two:
                        it is a promise about what a stack trace will show.
                        A word of its own rather than "%inline(never)",
                        since "never" names the empty type (section 3) and an
                        <attr_arg> takes an IDENTIFIER.
  %deprecated("...")    Warns wherever the thing it marks is used, and says
                        in its own words what to use instead. It goes on any
                        declaration.
  %test                 Marks a fn as a test: no parameters, "null" returned,
                        collected and run on its own rather than compiled
                        into an ordinary build.

"%repr", "%derive" and "%cfg" are the names to expect next, and none of them
is reserved: each needs something the language has not got — a layout story,
traits with code behind them, and a configuration to ask about.

Where a program begins. A suite is compiled from the one file named on the
command line, that file's module is the root, and the program starts at a fn
called "main" written at the top of it, taking no parameters. There is one
because a process has one beginning, and it is the root module's because that
is the file the compiler was pointed at — a "main" in something imported is a
fn called "main" like any other, and a suite whose files each held one would
be a suite of several programs and no way to say which had started.

Taking no parameters, because there is nowhere for an argument to come from.
A mangled name carries its parameter types (the "%symbol" entry above), so a
"main" written with them compiles to a different symbol and is a different
function; the one a process starts at is the one spelled with none. What a
program is handed by whoever ran it — the arguments, the environment — is not
in the language yet, and when it is it will be something "main" asks for and
not something it is handed for having the right name.

What it gives back is the exit status where that is an integer, and nothing
where it is not. An integer is handed on as it stands; anything else — "null"
above all, which is what a fn with no return type returns (section 3) —
leaves the program exiting zero. That is not tidiness. The register an answer
comes back in holds whatever the body last put there, so a "main" returning
nothing whose answer was read anyway would exit on a leftover: a number the
program never computed, different for a change nobody made.

"main" is not a reserved word. It is a name the compiler looks for, which
leaves it a fn like any other — callable from anywhere, including from
itself — rather than a word the language would have to stop somebody spending
elsewhere. A suite with none compiles; it is asking for a file to run that
turns the absence into an error, which is the right answer for a library and
the reason a library never has to say it is one.

Nothing written in the language calls the runtime's start-up. What a compiled
program actually begins at is an entry the compiler writes and links in
(src/link.rs): it notes where the stack begins — the one thing about the
collector's roots that cannot be worked out from inside it
(runtime/src/gc.rs) — and then calls "main". It is the only code in a Forte
program that nobody wrote in Forte.


==============================================================================
2. Declarations
==============================================================================

<fn_decl>. A body is a block, and a block is an expression (section 4), so a
function that falls off the end returns its body's value: "fn add(a: i32, b:
i32): i32 { a + b }" needs no "return". A <fn_body> of ";" is a signature
only, as in a trait.

A <return_type_opt> left out is "null" (section 3), which is a type like any
other — so "fn log(m: str)" and "fn log(m: str): null" are the same
declaration, and a bare "return" returns the value that type has.

<fn_sig>, "const". A "const fn" is one the compiler may run itself, which is
what lets a <const_decl> call anything at all: "const fn square(n: i32): i32"
makes "const AREA: i32 = square(8)" legal. It is a promise about the body,
checked where the body is written and not at the call. A "const" at the head
of a declaration is therefore the start of one of two things, told apart by
the word after it — "fn" makes a function and anything else a constant.

The two modifiers are written out as four alternatives of <fn_sig> rather
than made optional, which is why there is no <const_opt> or <unsafe_opt> to
name. A statement may begin with "unsafe" too (section 4), so an optional
"const" in front of one would have to be reduced away before the "unsafe"
after it is read — a decision needing the token past the one the parser is
holding, which is exactly what an LR(1) table has not got.

What may stand in the body is anything that can be worked out at compile
time, which is a wider licence than it sounds: a "let", a "var", an
assignment, a loop, an if, a match, a reference taken to something the body
made itself, and a call to another const fn. Mutation is not the difficulty —
mutating a local is just the evaluator keeping a value — and neither is
iteration. The single rule behind all of it is that the body must be closed
over its arguments: nothing may be read that the call did not bring in.

So what is refused is what reaches outside. A global "var", whose value
depends on when you look. A call to an ordinary fn, which has made no
promise. Anything doing input or output. A reference to a place that will not
exist until the program runs.

A const fn stays an ordinary function besides. Nothing stops it being called
at run time with arguments that were not known any earlier, and it is only
where every argument is a constant that the call folds away.

<fn_sig>, "unsafe". An "unsafe fn" is one whose caller has something to
prove that the checker cannot — that a run really holds the number of
elements it was said to hold, that what a "%symbol" names on the other side
does what its declaration here claims, whatever the function happens to
need. What the obligation is is written where the function is documented and
nowhere the compiler can read it; the word only says that there is one. So
calling such a function is itself unsafe, and the call has to stand inside an
<unsafe_stmt> (section 4).

Almost all of what needs this is what crosses out of the language: nothing
inside it is a pointer (section 3), so the reference rules hold wherever the
checker can see both ends, and it is a foreign declaration that puts a claim
where it cannot.

It sits after the visibility and in front of the "fn", so a declaration reads
"pub unsafe fn write(dst: *u8[], n: u64)", and "const unsafe fn" where
both apply. The order is the order of the <fn_sig> rule and not a matter of
taste: "unsafe pub fn" is not a declaration.

The word says nothing about the body. An unsafe fn is checked exactly as any
other fn is, and marking one buys no licence inside it — a body that wants to
call another unsafe fn writes the "unsafe" for that call like anybody else.
The two things are separate: this one is a demand made of the caller, and
section 4's is the discharging of one.

<where_clause_opt>. The same bounds a <generic_param> carries, said after the
signature instead of inside the angle brackets, which is worth doing once a
list of them stops fitting. It also says one thing the inline form cannot,
being written about a whole <type> rather than about a parameter being
introduced: "where Vec<T>: Show" bounds something that is not a parameter at
all, and has nowhere else to go.

Both places may be used at once and what they say is joined: "fn dump<T:
Show>(xs: &T[]) where T: Clone" asks for "T: Show + Clone" exactly as if the
two had been written together. Nothing has to be chosen between them, so a
signature is split wherever it reads best.

A <where_pred> has to constrain something the signature introduced, though —
its left side must mention a parameter somewhere inside it. "where Vec<T>:
Show" does and "where i32: Show" does not. The second is not turned away for
being false: "i32" may well implement "Show", since an impl is written about
any type and a primitive is a type like another (section 2). It is turned
away for being settled — it holds or it does not however the function is
called, so it asks the caller for nothing and the signature is no narrower
for having said it. Written by mistake it is a parameter misspelled; written
on purpose it repeats what the impl already said.

The "{" of the body still has to open on the line the clause ends, as every
body does — nothing continues a line into a "{" (section 7).

<binding_name>. A parameter, a variable and a for's loop variable are all
bindings, and any of them may be written "_" to say the value is deliberately
unused. A "_" binds nothing, so nothing can refer to it and two of them never
collide: "fn f(_: i32, _: str)" is legal where two parameters of one name are
not.

"_" is a token of its own, not an IDENTIFIER, and only as a whole word:
"_foo" and "__" are ordinary identifiers that happen to be spelled with
underscores, and bind as one. Nor is it an expression — it may not stand on
the right of anything, so a value is discarded by binding it, "let _ = f()",
and not by assigning to it: "_ = f()" is not an assignment.

<macro_decl>. A macro is declared with a word and invoked with a sigil, which
is the opposite way round from everything else and is the point: the
declaration reads as the declaration it is, and the use is marked wherever it
stands.

    macro twice($x:expr) {
        $x
        $x
    }

    let n = @twice(f())

A parameter is "$name:fragment". The "$" and the name are one token, as the
"%" of an attribute is and the "~" of a lifetime; the fragment is the word
after the colon, and it says what kind of thing the argument must be. The
fragments are an IDENTIFIER to the grammar rather than a keyword, and the set
of them is closed the way the attributes are: the compiler knows every one
there is, and a misspelling is an error naming what was meant rather than a
fragment nobody defined. There are three:

  expr      Any expression, and so no restriction at all.
  ident     A bare name. It is what a parameter standing in for a type asks
            for, a name being a named type wherever one is written.
  lit       A literal.

Three and not Rust's dozen, because an argument is an <expression> and these
are the three an expression can be. There is no "ty": "@m(i32)" does not
parse at all, a primitive being no <primary>, so a fragment that could only
ever be handed a name would be "ident" under another spelling. What that
costs is that no macro takes a primitive type as an argument, which is the
one thing the shape of an argument list rules out.

Where "$x" may stand is what its fragment decides, and the grammar admits it
in three places — an operand, a base type, a pattern — leaving which of the
three to the checker. That is the same division <const_expr> is written with:
the shape is the grammar's and the rule about it is not.

An invocation is an operand, "@twice(f())", and so stands wherever a value
does, a statement included — an expression is a statement already, so nothing
is written twice to say it. It is not an item: a macro cannot make a
declaration, and the pipeline is what that buys, expansion running before
`sema` looks at names but after the items are the ones the file was written
with (section 8).

Expanding is copying: the body's block, with each "$x" replaced by the
argument that stood against it, and the argument copied afresh at each use so
that a parameter written twice is the expression twice. A macro may invoke
another and may invoke itself, and nothing here proves either stops, so a
depth stands in for the proof and a program that runs past it is refused.
Nothing survives the pass — a declaration is dropped where it was written,
there being no macro of any kind past this point.

<impl_decl>. What an impl is written about is a <type>, and any type will do:
a struct, an enum, an array, a reference, a primitive. "impl i32" and "impl
Show for str" are both said, and nothing marks a primitive out as the language
does not hold one kind of type apart from another.

<impl_member>. An impl makes methods for its type and does nothing else. Its
members are <fn_decl>s and there is no other kind: no constant, no type, no
field. Whatever else wants to be grouped under a name is grouped by
<namespace_decl> below, which is the thing for it.

Two trait names mean something to the compiler, and neither needs a rule of
its own to be written. "Copy" says the type is copied rather than moved
(above, <var_decl>) and holds nothing:

    impl Copy for Point {}

"Drop" says what releasing one comes to, and holds the one method:

    impl Drop for Buf {
        fn drop(*self) {
            unsafe free(self.p)
        }
    }

The receiver is a "*self" because releasing writes to what it releases, and
that is the ordinary spelling for it — nothing about the declaration is
special, which is the point of putting it in a trait rather than in a form of
its own. What is special is the name, and that is a rule for the checker, as
the closed set of attributes is.

When it runs is the whole of what it is for. A value that moves has one owner
at a time, so the end of that owner is the one place a release belongs: a
local at the end of its block, a temporary at the end of its statement, a
field when the value holding it goes, and nothing at all where the value was
moved away first. Fields go in the order they were declared and locals in the
reverse of it, which is the order that lets a later one still refer to an
earlier one.

What is left for the checker rather than settled here: whether "drop" may be
called by hand, whether a type with a "Drop" may be returned from a "const
fn", and what happens to the rest of a value when a "drop" of one of its
fields fails to finish. A release that runs while something still refers to
the value is the aliasing rule's business (section 3) and is refused there.

<namespace_decl>. A namespace is a name put in front of a list of items. It
holds whatever a file holds — a fn, a struct, an enum, a constant, another
namespace — and changes nothing about them except how they are reached and
what "pub" means one level up:

    namespace limits {
        pub const MAX: i32 = 65535
        pub fn clamp(n: i32): i32 { ... }
    }

    let n = limits::clamp(x)

The "::" is C++'s, and reaching into a namespace is the second thing section
5 gives it: a type on one side of that separator or a namespace, never a
value. A namespace is a place to put things and not a type, so nothing is
ever an instance of one and no "." reaches into it — a "." is for what is
there at run time.

Nothing stops the two from meeting, since a namespace holds types and a type
holds variants: "shapes::Color::Red" walks a namespace, then a type, then
lands on a value.

Nesting falls out of the rule — a namespace is an <item> like any other — and
"pub" is needed at both levels to escape both, since a public member of a
private namespace is reachable only from inside it. "pub(suite)" on the inner
one is how to stop it at the edge of the suite while still letting the rest
of the suite reach it (section 1).

A nested namespace is what "super" climbs out of, and the enclosing one is
what it lands on:

    namespace shapes {
        fn circle() { ... }
        namespace poly {
            fn tri() { super::circle() }
        }
    }

A file is a module too, so a "super" written at the top level of one reaches
the module the file sits in, and "suite" reaches the root from anywhere.
Which module a path lands on is the resolver's to work out; what the grammar
settles is only that a root is a segment like any other, which is what lets
"super" repeat. It also leaves "a::super::b" parsing — a root in the middle
of a path, where it names nothing — and that too is turned down where a path
is looked up rather than where it is read.

<var_decl>. "let" binds a name that is read and never written, "var" one
that may be assigned again. That is the whole of the difference, and it says
nothing about when the value is worked out — a <const_decl> is what says
that. It is the same distinction "&" and "*" draw over a borrowed place
(section 3), so the two pairs are one rule learned once: the left of each is
read-only and the right of each is not.

Mutability is the root binding's, and writing reaches through whatever is
reached from it. A field or an element of a "var" may be assigned and one of
a "let" may not, so there is no marking a single field of a "let" writable,
and none weakening one of a "var" either:

    let  origin = Point { x: 0, y: 0 }
    var  cursor = Point { x: 0, y: 0 }
    cursor.x = 1                     // a place the writer may write to
    origin.x = 1                     // refused: its root is a "let"

What a "let" fixes is the binding and not the referent. A "let" of reference
type refers to one place for its life, and whether anything may be written
through it is the reference's own business — "&" says no and "*" says yes.
So "let p: *i32" never re-aims and still writes into what it refers to, which
is the pair section 5 lays out beside its "var" counterpart.

<gc_opt>. "gc" stands between the intro and the name — "let gc x", "var gc
x" — and says who releases the value: the collector rather than the scope the
binding was written in. It annotates the binding and not the value, which is
why it sits where it does and why the same value may be bound with it in one
place and without it in another. It is orthogonal to the intro: "let" and
"var" still draw the line they always drew, and "gc" says nothing about
whether the name may be assigned again.

What may stand under one is a heap value or a pointer, and nothing else. A
map or a set is the first — "let gc table = #{1: 2, 3: 4}" — and a "ptr" the
second, whichever of the two ways made it:

    let gc table = #{1: 2, 3: 4}     // a hash map, on the heap
    let gc seen  = {1, 2}            // a set, likewise
    unsafe let gc p = addr b.p       // a pointer, and still unsafe

Everything else is refused where it is written. A number, a boolean, a
character, a string literal, a tuple, a closure, a range and an "T[8]" are
all values that live where they stand, and a collector has nothing to do with
one; a reference is not a pointer either, since what it borrows is owned
somewhere else already — that is the whole of why it is not one. The word
would be saying who releases something that no one releases:

    let gc n = 1                     // refused: this is a number
    let gc r: &i32 = f()             // refused: this is a reference

The rule is held against the type where one is written and against the value
where none is, so "let gc p: ptr u8 = alloc()" is a pointer whatever "alloc"
turns out to return. Where neither settles it — a call, a name, a struct
literal, a named type — nothing is said until the checker knows what the type
is; a struct holding what it allocated is exactly the shape a container has
here (section 8), so a struct literal is where a heap value is most likely to
be rather than least.

"gc" and "unsafe" are unrelated words about the same statement. An "addr"
makes a pointer, which is both what a collector may hold and what the caller
has to answer for, so "let gc p = addr b.p" needs the guard exactly as it
would without the "gc" — the word buys no licence, and the two are written
together where both apply.

Both halves after the name are optional, which is what the <const_decl> below
is contrasted with. The type is inferred from the initializer where one is
written and written out where none is, "let grid: i32[ROWS][ROWS]"; a
<var_decl> with neither is a shape the grammar admits and the checker has to
answer for, as is a "let" read before it is given a value.

What "let b = a" does to a is move it. The value goes to the new name and the
old one is done with: reading a after that is refused where it is written,
and so is passing it, returning it or assigning through it. The same is true
of every other place a value is handed over — an argument, a return, the
right of an assignment, a field of a literal being built — since all of them
are one thing said in four places, and a value that has gone has gone
whichever of them took it.

    let a = Buf { .. }
    let b = a                    // a is done with
    f(a)                         // refused: a was moved to b

Copying is what a type asks for, and it asks with "impl Copy for Point {}" —
a trait with nothing in it, known to the compiler by its name as the six
attributes are. The primitives copy without asking, and so do "null", a
reference, and a fixed array or tuple every part of which copies; everything
else moves until it says otherwise. A type cannot have both a "Copy" and a
"Drop" (section 2, <impl_member>): a value that has something to release is
a value there had better be one of.

That default is the other way round from the language's first draft, and the
reason is what a move buys rather than what a copy costs. A value that moves
has one owner at a time, which is what makes releasing it at the end of that
owner's life a thing the compiler can do rather than a thing a reader has to
remember — and a copy of a thing that owns something is two owners and one
release too few. Which values those are is the checker's to work out, as
everything else about a reference is.

Nothing is written to move. "move" is a closure's capture mode (section 5)
and stays one; a move here is what the type already meant, and the line that
does it looks like the line that copies. The cost is Rust's cost, said
plainly: whether "let b = a" ends a's life is a fact about a's type and not
about the line, so a reader who does not know the type does not know what
the line did.

<type_decl>. "type MyType = i32" is a name for a type and not a type of its
own: nothing is checked about it that the type it names does not already say,
and two names for one type are one type. It takes generic parameters because
what stands on the right may want them — "type Pair<T> = (T, T)" — and a
lifetime is a parameter like any other, "type Ref<'a> = &'a str".

It ends with a separator, written or inserted, as every declaration does whose
last thing is not a "}". That is what keeps "type Grid = i32[8]" and the "[1]"
on the line below two things rather than one: an <array_suffix> could have gone
on, and the separator is what says it did not.

An alias is gone by the time anything has resolved through it. The tree the
checker is handed still has one, since a name has to be looked up before it can
be followed; what the checker hands on has the type it named and no alias at
all (section 8).

<const_decl>. A constant is a name for a value the compiler works out for
itself: "const MAX: i32 = 1 << 20". Both halves are required where a
<var_decl>'s are optional — the type, since nothing is there to infer it
from until the value exists, and the initializer, since a constant with no
value is nothing at all. It takes a <visibility_opt> and attributes like any
other declaration, and its ";" is inserted at a line break as any other
statement's is.

That is the syntax; the rest of it is a rule for the checker. <const_expr> is
written as an ordinary <expression> because the grammar cannot say which one
it wants: "evaluable at compile time" is a property of what the names inside
refer to, and nothing about the shape of the expression shows it. What is
meant to be accepted is the part of the language that is closed under
evaluation — literals, other constants, the operators over them, a cast, and
an array, map, set or struct literal whose parts are themselves constant,
together with if, match and block, which fold away once what they test is
known — and a call to a "const fn" (section 2), which is what makes a
constant able to compute anything at all. What is meant to be rejected is
anything that reaches outside: a call to an ordinary fn, a global "var", a
parameter, a reference to a place that exists only once the program runs.

A constant is not a place. Nothing assigns to it, and "*" of one is therefore
an error where "&" of one is not — a constant has no single home, being free
to be copied into each use. That is what the compiler does with one today,
and it does it in the checker: a name standing for a constant becomes the
constant's value there and then. What is folded is whatever the constant was
written as — the arithmetic, the shifts and the bitwise operators, the
comparisons, the casts between numbers, and a name standing for a constant
already declared. A "const fn" is not among them and is still a name for
somewhere; a global "var" now has data emitted for it, and both of those are
section 8's.

<enum_variant>. A variant is a case of a sum type: the types it carries are
its payload, got at by destructuring it in a match (<variant_pattern>,
section 5.2) and by nothing else. A payload is positional, "Move(i32, i32)",
or named, "Move { x: i32, y: i32 }" — the second where telling the parts apart
by order would be a mistake waiting to happen. A variant with no payload may
fix its discriminant instead, which is what makes an enum of plain constants.
The three are exclusive — a tagged value has no room for a chosen integer.

A <named_payload> is a brace holding field declarations, which is a struct
body in everything but name, and the lexer treats it as one: a type name
stands in front of it, so it is an entry body whose commas are written
(section 7).

A variant is named through its enum with "::" — "Color::Red",
"Option::Some(x)" — in an expression and in a pattern alike.

Every one of the commas in this section is written. The lexer synthesises
none of them, so a field, variant or arm per line needs a comma on each line;
only the last may go without, since a "}" closes the entry in front of it by
itself.


==============================================================================
3. Types
==============================================================================

<primitive_type>. "null" is in that list, and is the one type whose name is
also a value: "null" the type has exactly one of them, "null" the <literal>,
and a thing of that type carries no information beyond having arrived. It is
what a function with no <return_type_opt> returns, what a block with no
trailing expression is worth, and what a loop that nobody broke out of yields
(sections 4 and 5.1). There is no "void" — the type that would have been
called that has a value, and can be named, passed and stored like any other.

It is not a null pointer. A reference is never null (<ref_type> below), and
neither is a "ptr" (<ptr_type>), so nothing in the language reads "null" as
an absent something; it is the present nothing-in-particular. "let p: ptr T =
null" is refused, and refused as a type error like any other — the one value
of the type "null" is not an address, and there is no address it stands for.

"never" is its opposite, and the other type in that list whose values are
worth counting: it has none. Nothing is ever of type "never", which is why an
expression of it argues with nothing standing beside it — there is no value
it could disagree about. A "return", a "break" and a "continue" have it
(section 5), and so does a function that does not come back:

    fn panic(m: str): never

    let x = match c {
        1 => 5,
        _ => panic("no"),
    }

That types as an i32. The second arm yields nothing at all, so it agrees with
the first by having nothing to disagree with. Written "null" instead, the
same arm would be an error — a value that carries no information is still a
value, and an i32 is not one.

A body may not fall off the end of a "never": something in it has to leave,
or loop forever. That is a rule for the checker, as is everything about what
follows a call to one.

<ref_type>. "&T" is an immutable reference and "*T" a mutable one. Neither is
a pointer: no arithmetic reaches them, none of them is null, and there is
nothing to dereference. A reference stands for the place it refers to and is
read, called, indexed and reached into exactly as that place is, so "&" and
"*" decide one thing only — whether writing through it is allowed. It is the
distinction "let" and "var" draw over a binding, drawn instead over a
borrowed place.

"Exactly as that place is" reaches operators too. "a + b" over two "&i64" is
the addition of the two i64 they refer to and comes to an i64, and so is
"a + b" where only one of them is a reference. A "&&T" is read through twice,
one layer at a time, as everything about a reference to a reference goes.
There is no operator anywhere that compares two references as references:
what a reference *is* is not a thing the language talks about, and a program
that wants to know whether two names reach one place is asking a question
"ptr" is for.

And because "*" is everything "&" is and a licence besides, a "*" stands
wherever a "&" is wanted:

    fn read(p: &P): i64 { p.a }
    fn write(p: *P) { p.a = read(p) + 1 }

That goes one way only. A "&" handed where a "*" is wanted would be a write
licence made out of nothing, and is refused.

How many of each may stand at once is the other half of what the two say. A
place is reached either through one "*" and nothing else, or through any
number of "&" and no "*" — one mutable reference or many immutable ones, and
never both. That exclusion is what makes "&" worth more than a promise not to
write: a reader may take it that nothing moves under it while it holds the
reference, which is a fact about the program rather than about the manners of
whoever else is holding one.

It is a rule about places and not about names, so it reaches wherever a
reference is taken and not only where one is spelled out. A "*self" receiver
holds a mutable reference to the whole value for the length of the call, so
nothing reads that value while the method runs; a closure that
captures a name by "*" holds one for as long as the closure lives (section
5); and "f(&p, *p)" asks for both at once and is refused. The rule is the
checker's, and unlike the lifetime a signature leaves out — which is always
answered, and only ever answered too conservatively (below) — an aliasing
refusal lands on the line that asked for both, which is also the line with
nothing to write to argue it (section 8).

Two things it does not settle, both of them the checker's rather than the
grammar's. A field is a place of its own, so whether "*p.x" and "&p.y" may be
held together turns on how finely a struct is tracked once a reference has
been taken into it. And a view refers to a run rather than to one place, so
whether two mutable views of one array may be held when their ranges do not
meet is a question about what can be proved of ranges, not about what "*"
means.

The two compose with everything a type does: "&Point", "*i32", "Vec<&str>",
"&Map<str, *Node>". A reference binds looser than an array suffix, so
"&i32[]" is a reference to a run of i32 — the view of <array_suffix> below —
and an array of references is said with a <grouped_type>, "(&i32)[8]".

<ptr_type>. "ptr T" is an address and nothing else. It is the one type the
checker has nothing to say about: no lifetime, so nothing says how long what
it addresses is good for; no aliasing rule, so any number of them may point
at one place while a "*T" is out on it; and no promise that anything is there
at all. Everything "&" and "*" are worth is exactly what a "ptr" gives up,
which is why it is written with a word rather than with a third sigil beside
them — it is not a third kind of reference.

    fn write(dst: ptr u8, src: ptr u8, n: u64)

    struct Buf {
        p:   ptr u8,
        len: u64,
    }

It takes no <lifetime_opt> for the reason above: there is no lifetime to
sharpen, and "ptr 'a T" would be a promise the type is not making. It is
otherwise a type like any other and stands wherever one does — in a field, in
a signature, in a type argument, after the "as" of a cast — and binds looser
than an array suffix as a reference does, so "ptr u8[]" points at a run of u8
and "(ptr u8)[8]" is eight pointers.

What a "ptr" is not is null. There is no absent address, "null" is the one
value of the type "null" and not an address at all, and the "null" a C header
writes is answered here by a "ptr" that the code answers for or by a type
that says the absence outright. That is the same rule <ref_type> keeps, and
it is kept for the same reason: a type that admits one value nobody meant is
a type every reader has to check.

Where "ptr" is written is not itself the unsafe thing — a struct may hold one
and a signature may take one, as "write" does above. What needs the word is
making one and reaching through one, and "unsafe" is where both go
(section 4):

    unsafe let p = addr b.len
    unsafe {
        write(buf.p, src, n)
    }

A "ptr" is made by "addr" (section 5) and by a cast from another one, and it
is not reached by anything a reference is reached by: a "&T" stands for the
place it refers to and is read and written as that place, and a "ptr" is a
number until an unsafe statement says otherwise. It is reached through by
"deref p" for the first of its elements and by "p[i]" for any of them, both
of them places and both of them wanting the word:

    unsafe let first = deref v.at
    unsafe v.at[i] = x

"p[i]" is the index a run is written with, and it means here what it means
there: the address steps by the *stride* of the element and not by its size,
so a three-byte element with a four-byte alignment steps four. That is what
lets a container manage its own room without a spelling for the arithmetic C
does on a pointer, which is not written here: the one piece of it a container
needed was the index, and the index is already the language's. What the
elements are is what the pointer says, so a "ptr i32" steps four and a "ptr
T" steps by whatever the "T" of this instantiation turned out to be.

<receiver>. A method borrows its receiver by writing the reference on it:
"fn norm(&self): f64" reads the value and "fn scale(*self, k: f64)" writes to
it, the same "&" and "*" that mean this everywhere else. A bare "self" takes
the value whole and so moves it, which is what a method that consumes its
receiver is written with.

There is no "self: T". A receiver is the one parameter whose type nobody has
to say — it is the type the impl names — and the annotation was never doing
anything but repeating it. That is why the reference moved onto the receiver:
with the type gone there was nowhere else left to put it. What is still not
said here is which of the three a trait wants of the impls that answer it.

A reference to a reference is written as one expects, "&&T", though those two
characters are the logical operator as well. What tells them apart is what
stands in front of them: an operand — a value, or a type — makes "&&" the
infix operator, and anything else makes it two prefixes. So "a && b" is a
conjunction, while "&&i32", "f(&&x)" and "return &&x" are double references,
and the "&&" of a <type_bounds> list follows a type and so stays whole.

Note for implementers: only the first "&" is taken when the pair splits, so
the second is scanned on its own and "&&&*T" needs no rule of its own. This
is the ">>" of "List<List<i32>>" asked from the other side — one spelling
that two readings want, split on context the lexer already has. The one
reading given up is the conjunction of an open range, "1.. && x", and a range
is not a truth value anyway.

<lifetime>. How long a reference is good for has a name where one is worth
giving: "'a", the "'" and the name being a single token. That character
spells a character literal as well, and the two are told apart by looking
past the name: "'a'" closes and is a character, "'a" does not and is a
lifetime. The look is bounded by the name, which is what makes it worth
paying — it never runs past the identifier, where a "`"-delimited raw string
would have had to be scanned to the end of the file to answer the same
question. "'_" is the one with no name worth giving, and says a lifetime
belongs here that nothing else refers to.

A lifetime stands in the four places a type parameter stands in: among the
parameters of a declaration, "<'a, T>"; among its arguments, "Parser<'a>";
in front of what a reference refers to, "&'a str"; and on either side of the
colon of a bound, "T: 'a" and "'a: 'b". There is one list of parameters and
one of bounds rather than a list apiece, so whether the two may be
interleaved, and what a lifetime may be bounded by, are rules for the
checker. A <cast_type> takes none: nothing has needed to say one there.

    fn longest<'a>(x: &'a str, y: &'a str): &'a str

    struct Parser<'a> {
        text: &'a str,
    }

Writing one is not the usual case and is not meant to become one. A reference
with no lifetime in front of it is what nearly every reference is, and the
checker works it out from the shapes of the declarations around it — which is
what it did for every reference back when there was no way to say one. What
writing it adds is not an answer the checker was missing but a sharper one
than it would have reached on its own, said once in a signature and held to
by every caller.

What is worked out where nothing is written is one rule, and it never fails.
Every reference in a signature with no lifetime of its own gets one, and a
reference in the return type gets the shortest-lived of the ones the
parameters brought in. No signature is ever refused for want of a lifetime
and none ever has to be written: the shortest of them outlives nothing the
others do not, so the answer the rule reaches is always a sound one.

    fn pick(x: &str, y: &str): &str

    // The result is good for as long as the shorter-lived of x and y,
    // whichever of them that turns out to be at each call.

What the rule costs is precision, and it spends it at the call rather than at
the declaration. A "pick" that only ever gives back x is held to y as well,
so a caller whose y dies first is refused — the program that cannot be proved
is turned down where it is used and not where the thing that could not be
proved was written. That is the one place in the language a reader is sent
somewhere other than the line in front of them, and it is the price of never
having to write an annotation to get a signature accepted.

Writing a lifetime is how the precision comes back. It is never required and
is only ever a sharpening: "fn first<'a>(x: &'a str, y: &str): &'a str" says
the result outlives y, which the rule alone would not have said, and every
caller is held to that instead of to the shorter of the two. That is what
"'a" is for in a language that can always do without it, and it is the same
bargain as a type annotation on a "let" whose type was never in doubt.

A method is where the loss shows most. "fn name(&self, sep: &str): &str"
ties its result to sep as well as to the receiver, though what a method gives
back is almost always the receiver's. Rust spends a whole elision rule on
that one case; this spends none, and the method that wants it says so:

    fn name<'a>(&'a self, sep: &str): &'a str

A receiver takes a lifetime where any other reference does — a <ref_op> and a
<lifetime_opt> in front of the word, "&'a self" and "*'a self" — since the
alternative is a method that can be held to too much with nothing to write.
One rule that always answers, and a way to say better, is the trade — against
three rules that answer more often than not and a refusal when they do not.

<array_suffix>. Two suffixes, and what separates them is ownership.

"T[8]" is a raw fixed-size array: eight T laid end to end and nothing else —
no header, no indirection, and no length stored beside them, the length being
in the type. So it is a value like any other, and it goes where one goes: all
eight of them at once, whether "let b = a" moves them or copies them (section
2, <var_decl>), and the same when one is handed to a function or returned.
An array copies exactly when its element does, so an "i32[8]" copies and a
"Buf[8]" moves. Its size is known, so it may be a local, a field, a parameter
or a return type. "T[8]" and "T[9]" are different types, and neither of them
is "T[]".

"T[]" is a run of T whose length the type does not give. That makes it a type
of no known size, and nothing can hold one: no local, no field, no parameter
and no return may be a "T[]". It exists only behind a reference, and there it
is a borrowed view — "&T[]" one that reads and "*T[]" one that writes, the
same two spellings that say it everywhere else. A view is not a bare address
either: it carries the length its type left out, which is what lets it be
indexed at all and what keeps "not a pointer" true of it.

A reference to a fixed array is a view of it: "&i32[8]" is a "&i32[]" and
"*i32[8]" a "*i32[]", the length moving out of the type and into the value.
That conversion is the only one, and it runs one way — a view has forgotten
how many there are as a matter of type, so nothing turns it back.

Suffixes read left to right, outermost first, which is the order the indexing
that undoes them reads in: "i32[3][2]" is three arrays of two, an "a[i]" of
one is an "i32[2]", and an "a[i][j]" is an i32. An element must have a size,
so only the first suffix may be "[]" — "&i32[][3]" is a view of rows of
three, while "i32[3][]" would be three things of no size and is not a type.
A <grouped_type> changes which suffix belongs to what, and is how an array of
references is written at all: "(&i32)[8]".

An <array_literal> is a fixed array, so "[1, 2, 3]" is an "i32[3]". The
language has no owned array that grows: "T[8]" owns and does not grow, a view
neither owns nor grows, and anything else is a library's to provide (section
8). A library can now write one, which it could not before: a struct holding
a view and a length, moved rather than copied so that it has one owner, with
a "Drop" that releases what it allocated (section 2). That is what moves and
"Drop" were added for, and every container the language does not have is the
same shape.

<type_bounds>. Several bounds on one parameter are joined with "+", which is
the one thing that character does: "<T: Show + Clone>". The "&" and "*" beside
it are references and would read as one here, which is why the separator is
not either of them.

<grouped_type>. Parentheses around a type group it and do nothing else, which
is what gives the other reading of a reference and a suffix a spelling:

    &i32[]        a view of a run of i32
    (&i32)[8]     eight references, laid end to end
    &(i32[8])     a view of an array of eight, as "&i32[8]" already was

A group holds one type. What a comma inside those parentheses makes is a
<tuple_type>, and the comma is the whole of the difference: no comma, no
tuple.

<tuple_type>. "(i32, str)" is a pair and "(i32, i32, i32)" a triple. The
members are positional and in the order they were written, and two tuple
types are the same type exactly when their members are — a tuple is declared
nowhere and named nothing, which is what it is for: "fn divmod(a: i32, b:
i32): (i32, i32)" gives back two things without a struct written for the
occasion.

Two members at least. "(i32)" is an i32, the parentheses there having grouped
and nothing more, and there is no one-member tuple to write instead: the
trailing comma of "(i32,)" is refused, as no list of types in this language
takes one. Nor is "()" a type. The value that carries no information is
"null" (above), and an empty tuple would be a second name for it.

A tuple is a value like a fixed array is: it goes whole, copying where every
member copies and moving otherwise (section 2, <var_decl>), its size is
known, and it may be a local, a field, a parameter or a return type. The
suffixes compose with it as they do with anything — "(i32, str)[8]" is eight
pairs — and so does a reference: "&(i32, str)" reads one.

A member is reached with ".0", ".1" and so on (section 5), and the whole of
one is taken apart with a <tuple_pattern> (section 5.2).

<generic_args>. A call may name its type arguments, "foo<MyType>(x)", and
there is no turbofish: what tells that from the comparison "foo < MyType > x"
is the shape as a whole and not anything in front of the "<". A bare name is a
type and an expression both, so what stands *inside* the angles settles
nothing; what settles it is a matching ">" with a "(" after it. The lexer
looks that far ahead and hands the parser a "<" it has already decided about,
which is the division that tells a value's "{" from a block's.

The look gives up at once on anything that cannot appear in a type argument,
so "a < b && c > d" is two comparisons; it follows a "(" in but never out of
one, so "(a < b) > (c)" is a comparison in a group and not a call. A name a
declaration keyword introduced is the one name it will not open after, since
"fn sort<T>(xs)" reads from the "<" onwards exactly as "sort<T>(xs)" does.

One reading is given up for it, and it is the honest cost: "a < b > (c)" was
two comparisons and is a call with a type argument now. Parenthesise to say
the other thing. Nothing else in the language changes meaning.

Note for implementers: the lexer also guesses at a "<" it does not decide, to
know where a type argument list is open. It opens a context after an
IDENTIFIER and abandons it on any token that cannot appear in one. The parser
must therefore still be able to reject a "<" that turned out to be a
comparison. Inside an open context ">>" is emitted as two ">" tokens, so
"Map<str, List<i32>>" closes cleanly.

A "+", a "_", a "::", a parenthesis and an integer can appear in a type
argument as well — a bound separator, an inferred argument, a qualified name,
a group and an array size. A "." cannot, since a type path is all "::" now,
and one inside an open context says the "<" was a comparison after all. Nor
can a "&&" or a "||", which is what abandons the context in "a < 1 && b >> c"
now that the literal no longer does.

"&" and "*" can appear in a type argument, so neither abandons the context.
An "&&" cannot appear in one at all — a type argument never begins after an
operand, so the pair would have split — and a "&&" met inside an open context
is therefore proof the "<" was a comparison, and abandons it. That "a < b * c
> d" now keeps its context open where it used to lose it changes nothing the
parser did not already have to do, "a < b > c" having always kept it, and the
guess stays confined to ">>" splitting and semicolon insertion.


==============================================================================
4. Statements
==============================================================================

<block>. A block is an expression, and its value is the trailing expression —
the one left without a ";". A block with no trailing expression is "null".

The lexer inserts nothing before a "}", so the last line of a block never
gets a separator, and an expression written there is that trailing
expression. Write the ";" yourself and it is a statement again, and the block
is "null". That is the one place where a written separator and an inserted
one differ, and only because none is inserted there.

<statement>. A <declaration> there is a nested fn / struct / ...; a bare ";"
is the empty statement. "return", "break" and "continue" are not listed:
they are expressions (section 5), so each reaches a statement through
<expr_stmt> like every other one.

The ";" ending a <var_decl>, return, break or continue may be omitted before
a "}", as it may anywhere the writer leaves it out and no newline follows —
read those as "; or the following }". An <expr_stmt> is the exception: the
";" is exactly what separates a discarded value from the block's own.

<unsafe_stmt>. "unsafe" prefixes a statement, and what it says is that the
obligations the statement takes on have been met. The checker stops asking
there, and whoever wrote the word is answering for it.

Three things take one on: a call to an "unsafe fn" (section 2), an "addr",
and a read or a write through the "ptr" an "addr" gave back (section 3). The
word covers the whole statement it stands in front of and not the line it is
written on, so the block form answers for everything under its braces:

    unsafe {
        let p = addr b.len      // both are answered for
        write(dst, p, 8)
    }

It reaches down a statement and not into a declaration. A fn written inside a
guarded block is a declaration like any other and section 2 already says the
word tells a body nothing, so its statements answer for themselves — the same
rule that makes an "unsafe fn" no different inside than out.

What it prefixes is any <statement> at all, and that is the whole rule — the
familiar block form is not a case of its own but what the rule already says,
since a block is an expression and an expression is a statement:

    unsafe {
        let buf = malloc(n)
        fill(buf, n)
    }

    unsafe free(q)

The second is the one-line form: where a single statement is being answered
for, there is nothing for a block to hold and the braces are dropped. A
<var_decl> is a statement too, so a value is carried out of an unsafe region
by binding it there — "unsafe let buf = malloc(n)" — which is how a "let"
gets one at all.

"unsafe" opens no scope. It is a prefix and not a wrapper, so the "buf" above
is a binding of the block around it and outlives the line. The braces of
"unsafe { ... }" open one, as braces do, and that is the difference between
the two forms.

Its ";" is the prefixed statement's own, and may be left out in front of a
"}" like any other — which is the one place the word stands last, there being
no ";" in front of a "}" to end it with. There it guards the tail and leaves
it the block's value:

    fn elem(p: ptr i64, i: i64): i64 {
        unsafe p[i]
    }

That is what <block_tail_opt> means by taking an "unsafe" of its own: the slot
holds the block's value, and a guarded read is a value like any other. A
*declaration* in that slot is not one however it is written, so a block ending
in "unsafe let b = malloc(n)" is "null" as one ending in a plain "let" is.

<jump_expr>. The operand is the value of the loop the break leaves (section
5.1). Like a return's, it has to start on the same line: "break" can end a
statement, so a newline after it inserts the ";" and a value left on the next
line is a statement of its own.

if, while, for and match are expressions (section 5), so they reach a
statement only through <expr_stmt>: a statement is one whose value is
discarded. Nothing marks that position as special.

What ends one of them is the body's "}", and nothing carries on from there: a
block expression is a whole expression and never an operand (section 5.1), so
"match x { ... } - 1" is not a subtraction on one line either. Written across
two the "}" ends the statement and the "- 1" starts another, which is what
rule (e) of section 7 is for — without it the lexer would splice the two into
the one reading the grammar has not got.


==============================================================================
5. Expressions and patterns
==============================================================================

Lowest precedence first. Each level is written left-recursively, which is
what makes it left-associative; <assignment> is right-recursive and so
right-associative.

<jump_expr>. "return", "break" and "continue" are expressions of type
"never", the empty type (section 3) — no values at all, which is not "null"
and not anything else. Having no value, one never disagrees with a value
beside it, so a jump fits any branch: "let x = if c { 1 } else { return }"
types, the else arm yielding nothing because it leaves. That is the whole of
what being an expression buys them.

They sit here, beside <assignment>, and not among the <primary>s, so the
operand runs as far as the expression does: "return a + b" returns the sum.

<closure_expr>. "|a, b| a + b", and "|| f()" for one that takes nothing. A
parameter may be annotated where it cannot be worked out, "|x: i32| x + 1",
and the body is one expression — a block being an expression, "|x| { ... }"
is how a longer one is written.

A name the body uses but did not declare is captured, and how is worked out
per name, each taking the least the body asks of it. Reading one takes a "&"
of it and assigning to one takes a "*", which is the same pair everything
else uses and carries the same consequences: two closures that only read a
name may both hold it, and one that writes to it may share it with nothing.
A <move_opt> overrules all of that at once — "move |x| n + x" takes every
name it captures by value, so the closure owns what it holds and outlives the
frame it was made in. By value is a copy where the name's type copies and a
move where it does not (section 2, <var_decl>), which is the same rule every
other handing-over follows; the keyword names the capture mode and not the
transfer, and a "move" closure over a name that moves is a name the frame is
done with.

    var n = 0
    let show = || print(n)      // captures &n
    let bump = || n = n + 1     // captures *n
    let own  = move || n + 1    // captures n by value

This is the one place a reference is taken without being written, so it is
also the one place the rules of section 3 apply to something the writer did
not spell out.

The body extends as far as it can, which is what putting the rule here rather
than among the <primary>s says: "|x| x + 1" is a closure returning a sum and
not a closure plus one.

Note for implementers: "||" is the same problem "&&" was (section 3), and
takes the same answer — an operand in front of it makes it the disjunction,
and none makes it the empty parameter list of a closure. "a || b" is a test
and "let f = || b" is a closure.

<assignment>. The left side of an assignment must be a place expression
(name, field access or index) — a restriction the parser enforces, not the
grammar. A reference is transparent (section 3), so a name of type "*T" is a
place too, and what assigning to it does depends on what is assigned: a value
of type T is written through to the referent, and a reference of type *T
re-aims the name itself. Only a "var" may be re-aimed — a "let" of reference
type refers to one place for its life, as a C++ reference does — so the two
readings never both apply to the same binding by accident:

    let  fixed: *i32 = *a       // never moves
    var  cur:   *i32 = *a
    cur = 5                     // writes 5 into a
    cur = *b                    // re-aims cur at b
    fixed = 5                   // writes into a; nothing re-aims fixed

The type has to match exactly, and an assignment reaches one step and no
further. That is what keeps the two readings apart where a reference refers
to a reference, since the name's own type and its referent's are never the
same type:

    var q: *i32  = *x
    var p: **i32 = *q
    p = r                       // r is a *i32: q now aims where r does
    p = 5                       // rejected: 5 is an i32, not a *i32

So reading is transparent to any depth and writing is not — "p + 1" walks
down to the i32 and adds to it, while "p = 5" will not walk down to put one
there. Writing at the bottom of a chain is done through a name that stands at
the bottom, which is what "q = 5" is for.

<range_expr>. Non-associative, and every bound is optional: "1..10", "1..",
"..10", "..=n", "..".

<bit_or>, <bit_xor> and <bit_and>. "|", "^" and "&" between two operands,
which are "||", "^^" and "&&" done a bit at a time. They bind tighter than a
comparison and looser than a shift, so "a & mask == 0" is "(a & mask) == 0" —
the reading everyone means. C put a comparison tighter and has regretted it
ever since; there is nothing to be gained by repeating that here.

Among themselves the order is "&", then "^", then "|", tightest first, so
"a | b ^ c & d" is "a | (b ^ (c & d))". The three logical operators are the
same ladder over booleans — "&&", then "^^", then "||" — and the two ladders
sit one under the other: every bitwise operator binds tighter than every
logical one, so the bits are worked out before the booleans are.

Two of the three characters spell other things, and where one stands is what
says which. An operand in front of "&" makes it this operator and nothing in
front makes it a reference, exactly as "*" is a product or a reference by the
same test (see <unary_op> below). "|" is this operator only between two
operands as well: the "|" that opens a closure's parameters has no operand in
front of it, and the "|" between a pattern's alternatives stands where a
pattern does and not where an expression does. "^" is the exception and needs
no such test — nothing is written with a prefix "^" and no pattern uses one,
so a "^" is this operator wherever it appears and "^^" is always the logical
one. The lexer has told the doubled from the single before any of this
(section 4), so the grammar sees six operators and not three characters.

<logical_xor>. "^^", which is to "^" what "&&" is to "&". It is the one of
the three logical operators that cannot short-circuit: "a && b" is settled by
an a that is false and "a || b" by an a that is true, but neither side of an
exclusive or settles it alone, so both are always evaluated. That is a fact
about what the operator means rather than a rule the grammar carries.

"^=" goes with it, as "&=" and "|=" go with theirs, so the compound
assignments are one per binary operator that has a use for one. There is no
"^^=", and none of the logical three has one: "a ^^= b" would write a boolean
back to a name that has to have held one already, which is what "a = a ^^ b"
says without a spelling of its own.

The single "^" takes the "=" and the doubled one does not, so "a ^^= b" is
"^^" followed by "=" and not an operator. Nothing in the grammar takes that
pair, and the error says so.

<unary_op>. "&x" takes an immutable reference to x and "*x" a mutable one,
the same two spellings the types use (section 3). "*" is prefix and infix
both, as "-" already was, and where it stands is the whole of what tells the
two apart: "a * b" is a product and "*b" is a reference. There is no operator
for the other direction, since a reference already is what it refers to.

"*x" asks that x be a place the writer may write to — a "var", or a field or
element reached from one. "&x" asks less: any place at all, and a value with
no home of its own, which the compiler gives one. Both are rules for the
checker, as is everything about how long the reference stays good.

"addr x" is the third of them and the odd one: what it gives back is a "ptr"
and not a reference (section 3), so none of the above is asked of it and none
of it is promised. It takes a place, as "*x" does — there is no address of a
value that has none — and it takes nothing else, no lifetime and no claim
that x outlives what is done with the answer.

It is a word where the other two are sigils because it is not one of them.
"&" and "*" say which of the two references is wanted and the choice between
them is the whole of what they say; "addr" is a fourth thing standing where
they stand, and spelling it as a third sigil would have put it in a series it
does not belong to.

The word only ever stands inside an "unsafe" statement (section 4). That is
the one place in the grammar where a rule is about the word above it rather
than about what is beside it, and it is where the checker stops answering for
what happens next: an "addr" is the compiler being told that whoever wrote it
knows something it does not.

    unsafe let p = addr buf[0]
    let q = addr buf[0]         // error: `addr` needs an `unsafe`

"deref" is the other half and is a <unary_op> for the same reason: it is a
fourth thing standing where "&" and "*" stand and is not a choice between
them. What it makes is a *place* — the one operator here that does — so it may
be assigned to as well as read, and "addr" and "deref" undo one another:

    unsafe deref p = 1
    unsafe let n = deref p

It wants the same word, and of the two it is the one that can fault. An "addr"
takes the address of a place that is certainly there; a "deref" reads whatever
the address turned out to be, and whether it is still anything is exactly what
the checker stopped answering for.

A pointer that a call gave back needs no word of its own, the call having
been answered for already — "unsafe let p = malloc(n)" is how one usually
arrives, and the "unsafe" there is the call's.

<postfix_op>. Those are, in order: a field or method access, the "." of a
tuple member, a "::" reaching into a namespace, a module or a type, a call,
an index or slice, and the brace of a struct literal. The separators and the
brace are the ones worth naming as suffixes at all: either separator may
follow the other, so neither belongs to the name it reaches through, and the
brace hangs off whatever the two of them built — which is the whole of what
makes "shapes::Point { x: 1 }" sayable.

A "." reaches a field or a method, and which is decided by the name and then
by what was found. A field of that name wins where it could be the thing
called — a struct holding a "fn" is reached before an impl is looked in — and
where it could not be, the method answers. So a container declaring "len"
beside a "len()" is written the way it reads: "b.len" is the field and
"b.len()" is the method, and the only call the method takes from the field is
one the field could never have answered.

A tuple member is counted rather than named, so what follows the "." is the
number: "p.0" is the first of them and "p.1" the second. It is the same "."
and the same suffix — "f().0" and "ps[i].1" read as they look — and the only
thing special about it is lexical: a number written after a "." is a whole
one, so "t.0.1" reaches into the tuple in the first member rather than
naming a float (section 6).

<index>. A range is an expression, so a slice needs no rule of its own.

What a slice denotes is the run itself: "a[1..3]" is a place of type "T[]",
and nothing can hold one (section 3), so it is borrowed like any other place
— "&a[1..3]" reads those two and "*a[1..3]" writes to them. Indexing by a
single value is an ordinary place of type T and asks for none of that:
"a[1]" is the element, and "&a[1]" a reference to it.

".", "::" and ":". No rule gathers these into a path — the first two are
<postfix_op>s above and the third is not an operator at all — but the three
of them are the language's separators, and each reaches into a different
thing:

  "."   into a value, and into nothing else — a field, a method, "p.norm()".
  "::"  into a namespace, a module or a type — "limits::MAX",
        "shapes::circle", "Color::Red". A module is what an import reaches
        (section 1), and it is reached the way a namespace is and needs no
        separator of its own. An enum variant is what "::" reaches in a type,
        and the only thing: an impl holds methods, and a method is reached
        through a value with a "." like any other member (section 2).
  ":"   neither — it annotates.

So "shapes::Color::Red.name" walks a namespace, then a type, then a value.
The rule behind the first two is that "::" walks what the compiler knows the
name of and "." walks what is there at run time. A module is known by name
at compile time like everything else on that side, which is what settles it
onto "::" and leaves "." meaning one thing only.

A path may say where to start from, and the three words that say so —
"suite", "super", "self" — are segments of it like any other: "suite" is the
root of the suite, "super" the module above, "self" this one, and "super"
repeats. They are <primary>s here, so the "::" after one is the ordinary
<postfix_op> and nothing about a path needs a rule of its own —
"super::super::shapes::area(x)" is a name walked the way every name is. A
root reaches a type and a pattern the same way, through <qualified_name>.

The one place a path is written and none of this applies is an import's, and
only because a group and a glob are written there: "a::{b, c}" and "a::*"
reach names rather than values, and neither is an expression (section 1).
"::*" is one token and not two, which is what lets an import that globs end
its line like any other declaration (section 7).

<struct_literal_tail>. A "{" that opens neither a body nor a block, and a
place a separator is never synthesised (section 7, rule b). Every comma here
is written, so a field-per-line literal needs one on each line. It is a tail
because the name in front of it is not part of the rule: the literal is a
<postfix_op> on whatever expression named the type.

<map_literal>, <set_literal>. An array is "[...]"; a "{...}" whose entries
carry colons is a map and one whose entries do not is a set. A "#" glued to
the front of either brace makes it hashed — "#{1: 2}" is a hash map, "#{1, 2}"
a hash set — and orders nothing: a map or set written without it keeps its
keys in order.

"{}" is the empty map, as it is in Python, and "{,}" the empty set, which has
to be said some other way because "{}" is taken. No trailing comma is required
anywhere, so a set of one is "{x}".

Those two braces are also blocks, and the lexer is what tells them apart —
section 7. It takes a "," or a ":" at the top level of the brace as the mark of
a literal, and a ";" or a line break that ends a statement as the mark of a
block; where neither appears, "{}" and "{ x }" are literals where only a value
can stand and blocks where a statement could — with a closure's body the one
value position that takes the block. "{:}" is the empty map written so that it
is decidable in either place, as "{,}" already is.

The empty set is written, never derived from <empty>: that spelling is the
map's.

<tuple_expr>. "(1, name)" is a pair, and the comma decides here as it does
in a type: "(x)" is a <grouping> and worth exactly x. Two members at least,
and for the same reason — there is no "()" and no "(x,)".

The trailing comma *is* allowed at the end of a longer one, "(1, 2,)", which
is the one place a tuple and a tuple type part company. Every parenthesised
or bracketed list of values takes one — an argument list, an array, a set —
and none of the lists of types does, so each side keeps the habit it already
had. What the type side buys by refusing it is that "(i32,)" has no reading
at all rather than a second one.

A tuple is an operand like any other, so a member comes off it directly:
"(a, b).0" is a. The uses are the ones a type has (section 3) — a return of
two things, a pair passed as one argument — and what takes one apart on the
way in is a <tuple_pattern> (section 5.2).


------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.1 Block expressions
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

if, while, for and match are expressions, not statements. Each is a
<block_expr>, so each composes wherever a whole expression is wanted: bound
to a name, passed as an argument, returned, or left as the trailing
expression of a block. What each evaluates to:

  block       its trailing expression, or "null".
  if          the branch taken. Without an "else" some path through it
              yields nothing, which is "null".
  match       the arm taken.
  while, for  the operand of the "break" that leaves it. Every loop takes
              one — "break x" in a "for" and a conditional "while" as much
              as in a "while true" — and where none is given the loop is
              "null": a bare "break", or a loop that ends by itself with
              the condition going false or the sequence running out.

That every branch of an if, every arm of a match, and every "break" leaving
one loop agree on a type is a rule for the checker, not one this grammar
can state. A loop that can end by itself has "null" among the values it
yields, which is the same rule asked of the loop.

What a <block_expr> is not is an operand. It sits beside <assignment> under
<value_expr> and not among the <primary>s, so no operator reaches one from
either side: "match x { ... }.len()" and "if c { a } else { b } + 1" are both
refused, as is "1 + if c { 1 } else { 2 }". A <grouping> says all three —
"(match x { ... }).len()" — since parentheses make a <primary> of whatever is
inside them.

That is the price of the brace. A "{" after a complete expression can be read
as a body without looking any further, because a finished expression has
nowhere to put a block: "for i in 0.. {" needs no rule beyond that, the range
being done and the brace belonging to the "for". The reading given up is the
one nobody writes.

<header_expr>. The header — a condition, an iterable, a scrutinee — is an
ordinary expression with one restriction: no struct literal at its top level,
so the "{" that follows always opens the body. Parenthesise to get one in:
"if (Config { on: true }).on { ... }".

This is what lets the lexer tell "Point {" from "if cond {" at all, and so
which braces hold statements and take an inserted ";"; see section 7.

A map or set literal is exempt where the body it competes with holds
statements, since that body can hold no "," or ":" at its top level and a
literal that shows one is therefore not it: "for x in {1, 2, 3} { ... }" needs
no parentheses. The exemption stops at the bodies that do hold entries — a
match's, and a struct's and enum's in section 2 — whose commas look exactly
like a set's; a literal at the top level of one of those headers has to be
parenthesised, as a struct literal does. So must "{}" and "{ x }" anywhere in
a header: nothing in them says literal, so the body claims them.

<match_arm>. A block is an expression, so "=> { ... }" is covered by this too.


------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.2 Patterns
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

<pattern>. "_" is the wildcard; a bare IDENTIFIER is a binding. The two
differ only in that the wildcard binds nothing, which is why it may be
repeated in one pattern — "Pair::Of(_, _)" — where a name may not.

<variant_pattern>. Written out in full, "Color::Red" — a bare "Red" is an
IDENTIFIER, and so a binding that matches everything, not the variant of
that name. A variant carrying named fields is destructured with the braces it
was declared with, "Event::Move { x, y }", and a field may be renamed on the
way out, "Event::Move { x: col, y: row }".

<tuple_pattern>. A <variant_pattern> with no name in front of it: "(x, y)"
matches a pair and binds both members, "(0, y)" matches only those whose
first is 0, and a member no one wants is "_". Two members at least, as
everywhere else a tuple is written — "(x)" would be a pattern in parentheses,
and there is nothing for that to say.

It is the only way to take a tuple apart by binding, since "let" takes a
<binding_name> and not a pattern (section 2): "match p { (x, y) => ... }" is
how the two come out at once, where "p.0" and "p.1" take them one at a time.

<const_pattern>. A name that resolves to a constant tests against its value
instead of binding: with "const MAX: u16 = 65535" in scope, the "MAX" of a
match arm asks whether the scrutinee is 65535. Any other name binds, as
before.

That makes a pattern's meaning depend on what is in scope, which is the price
of it: adding a constant somewhere else can turn a binding into a test. It is
the reading that surprises no one, and it is what a <variant_pattern> would
do too if a bare name could resolve to a variant.


==============================================================================
6. Lexical grammar
==============================================================================

Produced by src/lex/lexer.rs. Listed for reference — the parser sees only
the token kinds.

This is where the notation turns over: the capitalised names are terminals
everywhere above and the start symbols here, and the angle-bracketed rules
below them are reached from nowhere else.

Four character classes are too large to enumerate, so they are given by
description instead of by a rule, and so appear in grammar.bnf only as
undefined nonterminals:

  <string_char>   any character except '"' and '\'
  <raw_char>      any character except '`'
  <char_char>     any character except "'" and '\'
  <letter>        any alphabetic Unicode character

IDENTIFIER. An IDENTIFIER is any such word that is not a <keyword>. "_"
is one of those keywords, so a lone underscore is the wildcard token and
never an identifier; "_x" and "__" are longer words, so they are identifiers
like any other.

<decimal_int>. A <decimal_int> must fit in an i64.

<base_marker>. Those are bases 16, 8, 3, 2 and 10 in turn, and a <base_digit>
is a digit valid in the marker's base.

FLOAT_LITERAL. The fractional part is only taken when a digit follows the
".", so "1.foo" is INT_LITERAL "." IDENTIFIER and "1..2" is a range.

Nor is it taken at all when a "." stands in front of the number: that number
is the index of a tuple member (section 5), and a whole one. So "t.0.1" is
five tokens and reaches into the tuple in t's first member, where the same
characters would otherwise be "t" "." and the float 0.1 — which is not
something the grammar has anywhere to put. Only the lone "." does this; the
dots of a range are their own token, so "0..0.5" keeps its float.

<int_suffix>, <float_suffix>. A number may name its own type: "5_u8" is a u8
and "2.6_f32" an f32. The "_" in front of it is the digit separator that
"1_000_000" is written with — dropped wherever it falls and never part of the
value — so "5u8" says the same thing and reads the same way. The suffix is
part of the number's token rather than one beside it, which is what makes a
space between them impossible: "5 u8" is two tokens and says nothing.

Only the twelve numeric primitives can be named this way. "bool", "char" and
"str" have literals of their own and nothing to ascribe, and "null" has one
value already. Nor does a tuple index carry one: the number after a "." is a
member's place, as just above, and a member has whatever type it has.

A float suffix on a whole number makes a float of it: "5_f32" is "5.0_f32"
with the point left out. An integer suffix on a number that has a point is an
error — "2.6_u8" names a type the value has not got.

The digits of a based literal are read as greedily as they ever were, so a
suffix spelled in them is no suffix at all: "0x1_f32" is the number 0x1f32,
"f", "3" and "2" being hex digits every one. "0xFF_u8" is a u8, "u" being
none of them.

Whether the value fits the type it named is not asked here. "-128_i8" is a
negation of "128_i8", which is out of range on its own and not in the tree the
"-" is in; the checker asks, where the sign is in hand.

STRING_LITERAL. A "`"-delimited string is raw: no escapes.

CHAR_LITERAL. A CHAR_LITERAL holds exactly one character.

<comment>. A block comment does not nest. <line_char> is any character except
a newline; <any_char> is any character at all.


==============================================================================
7. Automatic separator insertion
==============================================================================

A ";" may always be written by hand, and a "," always must be. The lexer
inserts a ";" at a newline when all of the following hold:

  a. The previous token can end a statement: an identifier, "_", a literal,
     "self", "null", "return", "break", "continue", ")", "]", "}", "..",
     "::*", a primitive type name, or the ">" closing a generic argument
     list. A "_" is there because it names a binding, so "let _" is as
     complete a declaration as "let x". "::*" is there so an import that
     globs may drop its ";" like any other declaration — which is also the
     whole of why it is one token and not a "::" and a "*": a bare "*" is
     owed an operand, so a line ending in one is a line still going.
     "super" and "suite" are in neither list: each opens a path and no path
     ends on one. Nor is the ")" of a "pub(suite)", which closes a
     visibility and not an operand, exactly as the ")" of a "%repr(C)" does.
  b. No "(" or "[" is open, and the innermost open brace is not an entry
     body — a struct, enum or match body, or a struct, map or set literal. A
     block is a boundary; an argument list is not, and neither is a list of
     entries, which is punctuated by hand.
  c. The next line does not begin with "}". A closing brace ends the last
     entry or statement on its own, which is what makes the trailing comma
     of a struct, enum or match body optional, and the semicolon of a
     block's last statement optional with it.
  d. The next line does not begin with a continuation: one of
     . + - * / % = < > & | , :
     or one of the keywords  else  elif  as  in.
  e. The previous token is not the "}" of a *block* — unless the next line
     begins with one of those keywords, or with a "," or a ":".

     Rule (d) alone is wrong once if, while, for and match are expressions,
     because a block's "}" then ends a statement in the very place rule (d)
     would carry the line on:

         match x { ... }
         -1

     A block expression is never an operand (section 5.1), so there is no
     reading in which those two lines are a subtraction. Rule (d) would
     splice them into one and leave the parser with an error; rule (e) is
     what makes them the two statements they were written as.

     Every other closer keeps rule (d) as it was, so a method chain still
     hangs off the ")" above it — and off the "}" of a literal, which closes
     a value and never a statement. Only a block's "}" is
     treated this way, and only against the operators: "," and ":" separate
     rather than operate, so they still continue the line and an entry body
     may still be written in the leading-comma style.
  f. A "->" does not precede the newline. "->" explicitly splices the next
     line onto this one and is consumed by the lexer, never reaching the
     parser. It cancels the insertion whatever the line ends with, so it
     reaches the lines rule (d) does not — the ones beginning with something
     that is not an operator:

         let n = f ->
         (x)

     That is a call. Without the "->" the same two lines are a statement
     naming f and a statement grouping x, both of them legal and neither of
     them what was meant.

     It was put there for rule (e)'s sake, to write the reading that rule
     gives up, and that use is gone: the grammar refuses a block expression
     as an operand however the lines are punctuated, so splicing "-1" onto a
     "}" only moves where the parse fails. Section 8 has what is left of the
     question.

Whether anything is inserted depends on the innermost open brace, of which
there are four kinds. Two are decided by what stands in front of the brace:

  - the *body of a header* — the first "{" at the bracket and brace depth its
    keyword was seen at, for "if", "elif", "else", "while", "for", "match",
    "fn", "struct", "enum", "trait" and "impl". Almost nothing else can be
    there, which is what section 5.1 buys by keeping a struct literal out of
    the top level of a header. It is an *entry body* under "struct", "enum"
    and "match", and a statement body under the rest.

    "unsafe" is a twelfth, and the only one that may head nothing: it
    prefixes any statement (section 4), so the brace in "unsafe p = P { x: 1
    }" is the literal's and not a body. It heads one where a "{" really
    follows it and whitespace is all that is in between, and nowhere else —
    the one place the lexer looks at a character to decide a keyword rather
    than at the keyword to decide a brace;
  - a *struct literal*, when no header claims the brace and a type name ends
    immediately in front of it: "Point {", "Vec<i32> {". Its fields are
    entries too, so it is an entry body as well.

The other two stand in the same places and have the same thing in front of
them — an "=", a "(", a "=>", a separator — so nothing there can tell them
apart, and the lexer looks inside instead:

  - a *map or set literal*, when a "#" is glued to the front of the brace, or
    when a "," or a ":" turns up at the top level of it. It is an entry body,
    and its "}" closes a value;
  - a *block* otherwise. A ";" at the top level of the brace says so, and so
    does a keyword only a statement starts with — "let", "var", "return",
    "fn" and the rest. This is the only kind that gets anything inserted.

Only the brace's own level counts: the "," of "{ f(a, b) }" belongs to the
call and the ":" of "{ Point { x: 1 } }" to the literal, so neither decides
the brace around it. A line break that would end a statement counts as a ";",
since that is what it is about to become — "{ f()" / newline / "g() }" is two
statements however it is punctuated, while between entries a newline means
nothing and the "," or ":" of a real literal is met before any line break
that could be read this way.

That leaves "{}" and "{ x }", which hold neither separator. Those are
literals where only a value can stand — after an "=", a "(", a ",", a
"return", an operator — and blocks where a statement could: at the start of
one, after a "=>", inside another block. So "{}" after an "=" is the empty
map and "{ x }" the set of one, while the same two written where a statement
belongs are an empty block and a block yielding x.

One position is a value's and takes the block anyway: straight after the "|"
that closes a closure's parameter list. A closure's body is the one place
where "in the middle of an expression" and "a statement could stand here" are
both true, and reading the brace as a literal there cost the commonest way to
write a closure — "|d: i64| { n = n + d }" is a block whose value is an
assignment, and it came out as a set of one thing instead. So the rule is
that a closure's body written in braces is a block, and the literal keeps the
spellings it has everywhere: "{,}", "{:}", and any brace with a "," or a ":"
in it, which is every literal of more than one entry. The one that has to be
written differently here is the set of one, "{x,}", which is the same comma
the other three already need.

A header is the one thing that overrules the look inside, and only as far as
it has to: it claims its brace whatever is in there, except that a body of
statements gives way to a brace that can only be a literal, since a "," or a
":" is illegal at its top level anyway. That is what lets "for x in {1, 2, 3}
{ ... }" be written without parentheses. A struct, enum or match body keeps
its brace unconditionally — its own entries are separated by commas, so the
look inside cannot tell it from a set.

So a ";" is inserted in a statement body — at top level and in the body of a
fn, trait, impl, block or match arm — and nothing at all in an entry body,
whose commas are written by hand (rule b). The kinds nest, and it is the
innermost brace that decides: a statement inside an arm's block is followed
by a ";", the "}" closing that block is followed by the written "," that ends
the arm, and a block inside a literal's field or a map's value is a statement
body again.

A separator is also inserted at end of input when (a) holds. "..=" never
ends a statement — it always needs an upper bound — while ".." does.

The header rule is the whole of it. Take it away and "if cond {" cannot be
told from "Point {" — both are a name followed by a brace — which is why the
restriction on <header_expr> is not a nicety the parser could drop.

Caveat: rule (c) is what gives a block its value. The last expression in a
block never gets a ";" inserted after it, so it becomes the block's trailing
expression (section 4). To evaluate something for its effect and leave the
block "null", write the ";" — the lexer will not.

Caveat: rule (e) is not a choice about which reading is common. It was one
when a block expression could be an operand; now that the grammar has only
the one reading (section 5.1), rule (e) is what keeps the lexer from handing
the parser a token stream that has none.

Caveat: a "{" at the top level of a header is the body, even where a struct
literal was meant. "if Cfg { on: true }.on {" does not work; parenthesise it,
"if (Cfg { on: true }).on {", and the inner brace is deeper than the header,
so it lexes as the literal it is. The same goes for a "{}" or a "{ x }" in a
header, which say nothing about themselves: "for x in ({y}) { ... }".

Caveat: a block used as a value has to hold a statement boundary — a ";", a
line break, or a keyword only a statement starts with. Every block worth
writing does, since a block of one expression is that expression; but
"let x = { f() }" is the set of one that it looks like, and "let x = (f())"
is how to say the other.

Caveat: "&" and "*" begin an expression now as well as joining two, and rule
(d) has always listed both, so a line starting with either continues the line
above it — the reading "-" has had all along. A statement that begins by
taking a reference discards it and is worth nothing anyway; where one is
really meant, end the line above with a written ";".


==============================================================================
8. Open questions
==============================================================================

Every question the token set raised has now been answered; what follows is
what those answers left behind. Each says what was decided and what the
decision does not cover, and the ones marked settled are kept because the
answer is not obvious from the rules alone.

  - <import_decl> is a "::" tree: a group under a common path, a group
    inside a group, a glob, and an "as" alias on the leaf it renames. An
    import is a namespace someone else wrote and is reached like one, which
    leaves "." meaning only "into a value at run time". Settled, and settled
    now in the compiler as well as on the page: an imported name reaches the
    file that imported it and no other file, an alias is what that file calls
    it, and a glob loses to a name written by hand. Where a path is looked up
    is section 1's -- a module is a file, and the longest prefix that names
    one is the module.

    What made this more than a resolver's job is generics. A "fn empty<K,
    V>()" written in one file becomes a body only where somebody says what
    "K" and "V" are, so a suite whose files were separate programs would have
    nothing to make it against. The whole suite is lowered into one typed
    tree for that reason, and the visibility written on a declaration is what
    holds it together: a name that is not "pub" is not reachable from another
    file, by an import or by writing its path out.

    A name that is not a legal identifier still cannot be imported, and
    nothing says what one would mean.
  - A path may start at "suite", "super" or "self", and a file is a module so
    that all three have something to mean (section 1). They are segments and
    not roots, which is what lets "super" repeat: pinning them to the front
    of a path wants the token after the "::" that follows a "super", and that
    is one more than an LR(1) table holds. Settled. The cost is that
    "a::super::b" parses, a root in the middle where it names nothing, and
    only the resolver can turn it down. A file's module is its path from the
    suite root, which is what stands in front of every symbol it compiles to
    -- so two files never name the same module, and two files may each
    declare a "stride" without either being the other's.
  - A receiver is "self", "&self" or "*self", and there is no "self: T": the
    type is the one the impl names, so the annotation only ever repeated it,
    and with the type gone the reference went where the type had been.
    Settled. What is not said is whether a trait may demand one of the three
    of the impls that answer it, nor -- since a parameter list is a list --
    that a receiver comes first and comes only in a method. Both are the
    checker's.
  - "pub" exports and "pub(suite)" exports as far as the suite, which are
    the two units there are (section 1). Settled. Whether a "pub import" of
    a name that is itself only "pub(suite)" is an error where it is written,
    or a re-export that quietly narrows, is not said.
  - A macro is declared "macro foo($x:expr) { .. }" and invoked "@foo(..)",
    the word declaring and the sigil using (section 2). Settled. "@" spells
    nothing else now that the attributes have moved to "%", so an invocation
    needs no lookahead to be one; "%" does spell something else, and is told
    from the remainder operator the way "*" is told from a reference -- by
    whether an operand ends in front of it. What is left is the whole of what
    a macro means: when it expands, what a fragment admits, whether a body may
    name anything its arguments did not bring in, and whether the closed set of
    fragments is the right set. An argument is an expression, so no macro can
    be handed a primitive type -- "@m(i32)" does not parse -- and that is the
    one thing the shape of an argument list rules out. There is no repetition
    and no second arm: "$($x:expr),*" is not written, so a macro takes the
    parameters it declares and no more. What is settled is when it expands,
    which is before anything reads a name: a macro is spent on the AST, and
    what "sema" is given has none left in it.
  - <attribute> is "%name" with its arguments, one per "%", the set of them
    is closed, and an unknown one is an error (section 1). Settled. The six
    are %symbol, %must_use, %inline, %noinline, %deprecated and %test;
    "%repr", "%derive" and "%cfg" wait on features that do not exist. Nothing
    says which declarations each of the six may sit on, beyond what its own
    description implies.
  - A name is mangled as "__F" and then each part written as its length and
    then its characters, and "%symbol" gives the exact name instead
    (section 1). Settled, and the parts are these: where the fn is declared,
    one part per segment; its own name; and one part for each parameter's
    type. A file is a module and a namespace nests another inside it
    (section 1), so both are segments and the file's come first — "area" at
    the top of "shapes.ft" is "__F6shapes4area", or two files could not each
    hold one. The two underscores are what nothing written in the language may
    begin with, so a mangled name collides with nothing a "%symbol" gave and
    with nothing on the other side of a foreign declaration; the "F" says a
    function, and leaves a letter free for whatever else has to be named.
    Four things follow, none of which section 1 spells out.
    The return type is not a part. Two fns may share a name and are told
    apart by what they take, and nothing tells them apart by what they give
    back, so a return type in the symbol would only make one no caller could
    work out. Nor is a lifetime: a region is worked out rather than written,
    and two fns differing in one are the same code.
    A type is one part however long its spelling runs, and the spelling is
    the language's own -- "&i32", "ptr u8", "i32[8]", "(i32,str)",
    "Vec<i32>", "gc str". The length in front of it is what lets a "<" or a
    space stand inside a symbol unescaped, which is the same reason an "_"
    may stand inside a name.
    A method's segments are the impl it is written in: the type, and the
    trait where there is one, since "impl Buf" and "impl Show for Buf" may
    both hold a "len" and only the trait tells the two apart.
    A generic parameter is written by its place and not by its name, so
    "f<T>(x: T)" and "f<U>(x: U)" are one symbol -- as they are one function
    written twice.
    What happens when a generic fn is compiled is settled, and it is the
    second of the two: something monomorphises, so each instance wants its
    own symbol and the arguments reach the name. An instance is the
    declaration's symbol with one further part for each type it was made
    with, written the way every other part is -- so "id<i32>" is the symbol
    of "id" and then "3i32".
    It is settled that way because a machine reaches a field by counting
    bytes, and how many bytes a "T" takes is not a question with an answer.
    Either every use is compiled separately with the types filled in, or
    every generic value goes behind a pointer so that they are all one
    width -- and the second is a decision about the language rather than
    about a back end. See src/mir/mono.rs, which is where it happens.
    A "%symbol" name is not added to. Nothing outside the language can
    predict a mangling, which is the whole of why a call out to C is
    written with one, so a generic given an exact name keeps it.
  - Every loop takes a "break x" and yields "null" where none is given
    (section 5.1), and "null" belongs to every type, so the operand of a
    break and the "null" of a loop that ended by itself always agree. Settled
    — and it is the billion-dollar bet: every type admits a value that
    carries no information, so every read of one may find it. Whether the
    checker tracks which of them can be null, and refuses the reads it cannot
    prove safe, is the whole of what is left.
  - "return", "break" and "continue" are expressions of type "never", the
    empty type, which is nameable and sits among the primitives beside its
    opposite "null" (section 3). Settled. A function may be declared to
    return it, and a body that could fall off the end of one is refused.
  - <struct_literal_tail>: "Name { field: expr }" is ambiguous with the body
    of an if, while, for or match. Resolved by <header_expr> (section 5.1):
    no struct literal at the top level of a header. Settled — the lexer
    relies on it to tell the two braces apart (section 7), so the parser
    inherits the answer instead of enforcing the rule.
  - A block expression is a whole <value_expr> and never an operand, so no
    operator reaches one from either side and a <grouping> is what says the
    other thing (section 5.1). Settled — it is what lets a "{" after a
    finished expression be read as a body without looking past it, which is
    the lexer's whole footing in section 7. What it leaves behind is rule
    (f): "->" was put in to write the reading this gives up, there is no
    longer one to write, and whether the general line splice earns its keep
    on the cases rule (d) misses is not decided.
  - <type_bounds> joins bounds with "+", which leaves "&" and "*" to the
    references that had claimed them. Settled. Nothing is said about where a
    bound may be written besides a <generic_param> and a <where_pred>.
  - <match_arm> alternation uses "|", which is a token of its own now, split
    from "||" by what stands in front of it (section 5). Settled. A closure
    takes the same character, and the two never meet.
  - The two array suffixes are "T[8]" owned and fixed and "T[]" a run only a
    reference can hold (section 3). Settled. What that leaves out is the
    owned array that grows: there is none, and "Vec<T>" as an ordinary
    generic type is the assumption everywhere here that one is wanted.
    Nothing says how it is built or how it hands out the "&T[]" of its
    contents.
  - A map and a set are "Map<K, V>" and "Set<T>", and the hashed kinds are
    types of their own, "HashMap<K, V>" and "HashSet<T>" — so which one you
    named says how it behaves, and a "#{" literal builds the hashed one.
    Settled. Neither is sugared in a type the way the suffixes sugar an
    array, and nothing says what a "{" literal with no annotation builds.
    Where the type is *found* is settled too, and it is the prelude: these
    four and "Range" are bound in every file without an import, each from the
    module named after it (section 1). What is still true is that no library
    here declares "Map" or "Set" — the ordered pair — so those two literals
    have a spelling and nothing to build, which is the same gap as before and
    is now the only one left of it.
  - Whether "{}" and "{ x }" are literals or blocks is decided by the position
    they stand in (section 7), which is a choice about which reading is
    common. The other reading always has a spelling: "(f())" for the block,
    "{:}" and "{,}" for the empty map and set. Settled.

    One position had the choice made the wrong way round and has been turned
    over: the body of a closure. It is a value position, so a brace there was
    a literal, and "|d: i64| { n = n + d }" — a closure whose body is one
    statement, which is most of them — was the set of one thing it also looks
    like, and came out as "no type is called `Set`". The commonest spelling of
    a construct is not the one to lose an ambiguity to. So the brace after the
    "|" that closes a parameter list opens a block, and the set of one keeps a
    spelling there as everything else already did: "{x,}".
  - <enum_variant> carries a positional payload or a named one (section 2).
    Settled. A named payload is a struct body in all but name, and nothing
    says whether the two should be one thing.
  - "_" is a pattern, a binding name, an inferred type argument and a digit
    separator (sections 2 and 6). Settled — the last two are what Rust does
    with it. The lexer no longer abandons a generic context on one, so
    "Vec<_>" holds together; what an inferred argument may be inferred *from*
    is a rule for the checker.
  - A "*T" is re-aimed by assigning a reference to it and written through by
    assigning a value; the type decides, matching exactly and reaching one
    step, and a "let" of reference type never moves (section 5). Settled, and
    settled now in the compiler as well as on the page. Only the reading half
    of transparency had been built: an operator over a reference reached what
    it referred to, and an assignment to one reached nothing, so every write
    through a reference was turned down — "`i64` cannot be assigned to
    `*i64`", naming the two types that were never meant to match. A "swap"
    written the way section 3 says to write one did not compile.

    What it leaves is the asymmetry: reading walks a chain of references to
    the bottom and writing reaches one step, so the two are transparent to
    different depths and only the reader of a long chain has to notice. That
    is a decision and not an omission — a write that went as far as it had to
    would make "p = q" mean something that depends on how deep "p" happens to
    be — but it is worth saying that the compiler now draws the line exactly
    there rather than nowhere.

    The half of the rule that is about the binding rather than the type is
    enforced now too, and it had been missing everywhere rather than only
    here: nothing checked immutability at all, so "let x: i64 = 1" followed
    by "x = 2" compiled, and a "let" meant nothing. What it is held to is the
    predicate a "*" was already held to — taking a mutable reference and
    assigning are the two ways of writing, and they ask a place the same
    question, so they ask it in one place. Mutability is the root binding's
    and reaches through whatever is reached from it, which is what refuses
    "origin.x = 1"; a "let" of "*" reference type still writes through, since
    what a "let" fixes is the binding and not the referent.

    Two things fell out of writing it. An assignment through a reference had
    no place the checker could name — the "Deref" step was put in only where
    something was reached *after* it — so writing through an immutable
    reference was accepted, "&" and "*" differing in nothing so long as the
    write went no deeper than the referent. And the only code in the suite
    the new rule turned down was two tests that were writing through a
    "&T[]", which section 3 says is the one that reads.
  - How long a reference stays good has a spelling, "'a", and never needs
    one: a return borrows for the shortest-lived of what the parameters
    brought in, which is always sound, so inference answers every signature
    and refuses none (section 3). Settled, and it is the one place the
    language is further from Rust than it looks. Rust has three elision
    rules that answer most signatures and refuse the rest until a person
    writes the answer; this has one that answers all of them, conservatively,
    and lets a person write a better one. Nothing has to be annotated to
    compile.
    A type that carries a reference carries a region the same way, and a
    declaration that names no lifetime for the references in it is read as
    declaring one for each: "struct Held { it: &i32 }" takes a region, so
    "Held<'a>" names it and a bare "Held" is handed one, and the two promise
    the same thing. A declaration reached from itself is the one shape with
    no finite number of them — "struct A { b: &B }" beside "struct B { a: &A
    }" adds one every turn round — and what comes of such a type is held to
    every parameter, which is sound and blunter than it has to be.
    The cost is where the refusal lands. Too conservative a signature is
    turned down at the call and not at the declaration, so the message points
    at a use and the fix belongs somewhere else — the one place in the
    language a reader is sent off the line in front of them. A method feels
    it most, its result being tied to every parameter where it almost always
    meant the receiver; Rust's third rule is exactly that case, and is not
    here. What is left is whether the trade is the right one, and it is the
    only piece of this that a body of real programs would settle rather than
    an argument.
    The spelling is Rust's too, and so is what it costs: "'" already spells a
    character literal, so a "'" is told from one by looking past the name for
    a closing quote. That look is bounded by the identifier and no more, which
    is what makes it affordable — a delimiter with no bound, "`" among them,
    could not be told apart the same way at any price.
  - A place is reached through one "*" or through any number of "&", and
    never both at once (section 3). Settled. It is Rust's rule and it buys
    what Rust buys with it: an "&" is a guarantee that nothing moves
    underneath, so what may be cached, reordered or handed to another thread
    follows from the type rather than from a convention. The cost is harder
    than the lifetime entry's, and for the reason that entry is easy: there
    a signature is always answerable and "'a" only sharpens the answer, while
    here a program refused for aliasing is refused with nothing to write at
    all — a "'a" says which references are the same one, and the language has
    no way to say which are not. The two are otherwise one question asked
    twice, since a reference that outlives its referent and one that aliases
    a "*" are both a place reached two ways at once. How long a borrow is in
    hand is settled with them: it lasts from where it is taken to the last
    place anything can reach through it, which is where the name holding it
    is last read, and a name read inside a loop is read for all of the loop.
    A borrow that reached no name is a temporary and goes with its statement,
    and what a call gives back is what says whether one reached: "len(&x)"
    hands back a number and holds nothing, so the borrow is over with the
    line.
    What is left is how finely a place is tracked: whether two fields of one
    struct, or two mutable views of one array whose ranges do not meet, are
    one place or two.
  - A lifetime is written "'a", the elision rule never fails, and writing one
    "is only ever a sharpening" (section 3). Settled, and the checker holds a
    caller to every parameter the callee's result is tied to, and to every
    "'a: 'b" and "T: 'a" the signature was written with. A receiver takes one
    where any other reference does — <receiver> is "self" or a <ref_op> and a
    <lifetime_opt> in front of the word — so "the method that wants it says
    so" is a thing that can be said. A lifetime takes no type argument at a
    call: "<'a, T>" wants one and not two, a region being worked out by the
    pass that compares regions and not by unification. What is left open is
    the other direction: nothing says whether a lifetime declared among the type
    parameters but named nowhere in the signature should be warned about, the
    way an unused type parameter would be.
  - An array of references is "(&i32)[8]", a <grouped_type> being what says
    which of the two a reference and a suffix make (section 3). Settled. The
    same parentheses make a tuple when a comma is inside them and group when
    none is, so "(&i32)[8]" is eight references and "(&i32, u8)[8]" eight
    pairs; the two never compete, a group of one being the only thing a
    <grouped_type> is.
  - A tuple is two or more types or values in parentheses, positional,
    declared nowhere, reached into with ".0" and taken apart with a
    <tuple_pattern> (sections 3, 5 and 5.2). Settled — including that there
    is no "()" and no one-member tuple, that "null" is what an empty one
    would have been, and that the trailing comma is a value's and not a
    type's. Two things it does not say. A tuple has no place to be
    destructured but a match arm, since a <var_head> takes a <binding_name>
    and not a pattern, so "let (a, b) = p" is not written. And a tuple on
    the left of an "=" is a <tuple_expr> like any other, so "(a, b) = (b, a)"
    parses; whether the swap it looks like is what it means, or whether the
    parser refuses it as it refuses any other left side that is not a place,
    is not decided here.
  - A constant is evaluable when a "const fn" is what it calls, and a const
    fn may use anything workable out at compile time — locals, mutation,
    loops, references to its own values, other const fns — the one rule
    being that its body is closed over its arguments (section 2). Settled.
    What is left is the evaluator's own limits: how deep a recursion or how
    long a loop it will sit through before giving up, and what it says when
    it does.
  - There are no associated constants, and "Color::MAX" names nothing: an
    impl makes methods for its type and holds nothing else (section 2). A
    constant that wants a name in front of it goes in a <namespace_decl> and
    is reached with "::", "limits::MAX". Settled.
  - <impl_decl> takes its own <generic_params_opt>, so "impl<T> Stack<T>"
    parses and a method may use the parameter its struct was declared with.
    Settled, and settled now in the compiler as well as on the page. It had
    only ever parsed: the parameter was in scope nowhere, so "impl<T> Box<T>"
    was "no type is called `T`" against its own header and again against every
    member, and there was no way to write a method on a generic type at all.
    That is why the standard library is free functions and says so in three
    files — "a method on a generic type wants an `impl<T>`, and the parameter
    an `impl` introduces is not yet in scope inside its own members".

    A parameter has to be in scope in three places, and they are three
    different passes: the impl's own subject, where "Box<T>" is resolved; each
    member's signature; and each member's body. What makes the rest of the
    compiler need to know nothing about any of it is that the impl's
    parameters are *prepended* to each member's own rather than kept beside
    them — a method of "impl<T> Box<T>" written "fn map<U>(..)" has two
    parameters, "T" at 0 and "U" at 1, and one list is what a "Ty::Param"
    indexes into.

    What answers them at a use is the receiver, and only the receiver: a
    method call writes no type arguments, there being nowhere to write them.
    So "b.get()" on a "Box<i32>" is what says the "T" of the impl is an
    "i32", and it says it twice — once in the checker, where the receiver is
    unified against the type the declaration wrote, and once in "mir::mono",
    where the same match recovers the argument the instance is made with. A
    parameter that appears in the receiver and nowhere else — the "B" of
    "impl<A, B> Pair<A, B>" writing "fn first(&self): A" — is found by that
    and by nothing else.

    Whether the parameters of the impl and those of a method inside it may
    share a name is answered, and the answer is no. A parameter is found by
    name and the first of that name answers, so a member re-declaring the
    impl's "T" would be declaring one nothing could ever write — and what
    came of it before it was refused was a type the checker never settled and
    a program that would not link. It is a message now, and a member has to
    call its own something else.
  - A "." reaches a field or a method, and a field of the same name wins where
    it could be the thing called (section 5). Settled, and settled the other
    way round from how it started: the field used to win outright, which made
    a method of that name unreachable and said "`i32` is not a fn" about a
    name the reader was not talking about. A container declaring "len" beside
    a "len()" is the ordinary case and not a clever one, and it did not
    compile.

    Nothing lost a reading by the change. Where the field is callable it still
    wins, so "b.f(x)" is still the field; where it is not, the method takes
    only a call that could not have worked, and "b.len" still reads it. A
    parenthesis says nothing either way — a <grouping> does not survive
    lowering, so "(b.len)()" and "b.len()" are the one expression. What is
    still not said is how to reach the *method* where a callable field of that
    name shadows it, which is the one direction with no spelling.
  - <array_suffix> takes a <const_expr>, so a constant gives an array its
    size: "i32[ROWS]" parses and "i32[rows()]" does too when "rows" is a
    const fn. Settled. Whether two array types whose sizes are written
    differently but compute alike are the same type is not said.
  - A <const_pattern> tests and any other name binds (section 5.2), which
    makes what a pattern means depend on what is in scope. Settled. Nothing
    says whether the checker should warn when a binding shadows a constant of
    the same name, which is where the surprise would show up.
  - A closure captures a name by "&" where it reads one and by "*" where it
    assigns to one, worked out per name, and a "move" closure captures every
    name by value instead (section 5). Settled, and so is what follows from
    it: a closure that captures by reference cannot outlive what it captured,
    and "move" is the only thing that lets one be returned. A name captured
    by reference may not be handed away from inside the closure either — the
    value is still the enclosing frame's, and there is no capture mode short
    of "move" that takes it. One call is told from the next by what the fn
    type says: a "move" closure that gives away what it captured is a "once
    fn", and calling one takes it, so a second call is a use of something
    that has gone. That is the work Rust's "FnOnce" and "FnMut" do, written
    as a word in front of a type rather than as a trait. The keyword
    predates values that move and keeps its meaning beside them: it says how
    a name is captured, where a move says what happens to the value once it
    is. A closure's type is written "fn(i32, str): bool" — the shape a
    <fn_decl> has with the names gone — so one may be a parameter, a return
    type or a field, and both halves of the rule apply. It is a <type> and
    not a <base_type>, so "fn(): i32[8]" gives back eight numbers and
    "(fn(): i32)[8]" is eight closures. The one place it may not stand bare
    is the subject of a "where": after "fn(...)" a ":" would be its return
    type and the predicate's colon at once, and the parentheses say which.

    How one is *called* was the half this left open, and it is settled now.
    A fn value is two words — where the code is and where the captures are —
    and a declared fn handed round as a value is the same two words with
    nothing in the second, so one call has to reach either. So: the captures
    go over as one more argument, after the written ones. Last and not first,
    because an argument the callee never declared is harmless where it is
    last and shifts every other one where it is first — no ABI here has the
    callee count what it was passed. A closure that captured something
    declares a parameter for it, a closure that captured nothing declares
    none, and a declared fn ignores a word it was handed. The environment
    itself is a run of addresses, one per capture, in the order they were
    taken: the slot's own address where the name was captured by reference,
    so that reading and writing both reach the name outside through the one
    address; and the address of a copy the collector holds where it was
    captured by value, since a "move" closure owns what it took and is the
    one that may outlive the frame it took it from.

    What this leaves is a closure that captured nothing being told from a
    declared fn only by which symbol the first word names — nothing at a call
    site knows which it has, which is what makes the convention have to suit
    both. The cost is one argument register per indirect call.
  - A value moves unless its type says "impl Copy", and a type says what
    releasing it comes to with "impl Drop" (section 2). Settled. Neither
    needs a rule of its own -- an empty impl and a one-method impl are both
    written already, and it is the two names the compiler knows, as it knows
    the six attributes. Moving by default is the way round that makes a
    release something the compiler can place rather than something a reader
    has to remember, and it is what finally lets a library write the growing
    container section 3 says is a library's to write. The cost is Rust's
    cost twice over: whether a line moves or copies is a fact about a type
    and not about the line, and every program written before this one was
    settled meant something slightly different. Where a release goes is
    settled and placed: a local at the end of its block, a parameter at the
    end of the body, in the reverse of the order they were bound, and none
    at all for a slot the source moved away from. A slot moved away on one
    path and not another carries the answer instead of settling it — a
    "bool" beside the slot, false where nothing is in it and true where
    something is, with the release behind a branch on it — since which path
    ran is not known until it has run. What is left is whether "drop" may be
    called by hand, what a "Drop" means for a "const fn", and how much of a
    value survives a "drop" that does not finish.
  - "Copy" and "Drop" are traits like any other and are declared like any
    other: what the compiler knows is the two names, not the two
    declarations. An "impl Drop for Buf" with no "trait Drop" in scope is
    refused, so a type cannot look as if it has a release and have none.
  - "unsafe" is a modifier on a <fn_decl> and a prefix on a <statement>, and
    those are its only two places (sections 2 and 4). Settled — the statement
    form is one rule because a block is already a statement, so the braces
    are the writer's choice and not the grammar's. Two things it does not
    say. There is no unsafe *expression*: "let b = unsafe { malloc(n) }" is
    not written, "unsafe let b = malloc(n)" is, and whether the first should
    also be allowed is open.

    The one place the word is on something that is also a value is the tail of
    a block, which <block_tail_opt> has always admitted — there is no ";" in
    front of a "}", so that slot is where an "unsafe" stands last or nowhere.
    It took the value away until now: the word made a statement of what it
    prefixed, the block yielded "null", and because "null" belongs to every
    type nothing was said about it — "fn read(p: ptr i64): i64 { unsafe
    p[0] }" compiled and gave back nought. It guards the tail and leaves it
    the value now, which is what every container reaching through its own
    pointer wanted. It is still no unsafe expression: the word may not be
    written on an operand, and the tail of a block is not one. And the body of an unsafe fn is checked like
    any other, so an unsafe call inside one writes the word — the opposite
    convention, where the marking on the signature reaches the whole body, is
    the alternative that was not taken.
  - "ptr T" is a raw pointer, "addr x" makes one and "deref p" reads it back,
    and all three are section 3's. Settled as far as the grammar goes: "ptr"
    is a type and not a third <ref_op>, "addr" and "deref" are <unary_op>s and
    not sigils, and none of them takes a lifetime.

    "deref" settles the first of the three things this entry used to say were
    open. It is a *place* and not merely a value — it may be written as well
    as read, which is what section 4 already assumed when it named "a read or
    a write through the ptr an addr gave back" among the three things an
    "unsafe" answers for. So "addr" and "deref" are a pair: one turns a place
    into an address and the other turns an address back into a place, and
    neither may be written without the word.

    Indexing settles the second. "p[i]" is a place, under the same "unsafe"
    and stepping by the same stride an indexed run steps by (section 3), and
    it is the whole of the pointer arithmetic a container needs: a "Vec" is a
    "ptr T" and a length, and "v.at[i]" reaches the i'th of them. The general
    arithmetic — "p + n" as an address rather than a number — is still not
    written, and now has one caller fewer asking for it.

    The third was answered without being noticed. "p as i64", "n as ptr u8"
    and "p as ptr i32" all lower to a conversion of the width they are, which
    is what a cast between a "ptr" and an integer *is* on every machine here,
    so the entry claiming there was no spelling for it was reading the
    grammar and not the checker. It is written down now because a claim that
    something is missing is worth no more than the last time somebody tried
    it.

    So none of the three is open, and the one answer given in advance still
    holds: none of them will be null. A "ptr" admits no absent address, as a
    reference admits none.
  - Nothing at file scope had anywhere to live, and both halves of what was
    wanted are now written. A constant folds whatever it was written as: the
    arithmetic, the shifts and the bitwise operators over integers, the
    comparisons, the casts between numbers, and a name standing for a const
    already declared — so "const N: i64 = 6 * 7" puts a 42 where the name was
    and reaches no symbol at all. And what is left is a global, which cannot
    fold because it is a *place*; each one is given as many bytes as its type
    is, in a ".data" segment beside the constant pool and deliberately not in
    it, since the pool is ".rodata" and a store into that faults. A global
    nothing initialised starts as nought, and one nothing mentions is still
    given its room: a global is a declaration and not a use.

    What this turned up is worth having written down, because none of it was
    about globals and all of it was found by them. Two passes had been
    treating a global as a value rather than as a place. "sir::opt" shared
    two reads of one across a store between them — it asks whether two
    instructions with the same operands make the same value, and the answer
    had been yes for every way of naming a declaration, which is true of a fn
    and of a const and false of the one that is a place. And the same pass
    dropped every store to a global but the last in its block: a store is
    dead if nothing reads it before it is written over, and the list of
    things that read had a load on it and not the naming of a global, which
    is how a global is read. Both were unreachable before, no program holding
    one having ever linked.

    The same two mistakes were being made about "p[i]", and there nothing had
    been unreachable — every container that manages its own room is written
    out of it. A read of an element is the one read in the SIR that does not
    name the address it reads: it is an instruction of its own, and it sat in
    the same list of things that "make the same value from the same operands",
    which is a list for arithmetic. So two reads of one element with a write
    between them came out as one read, and a store an element read was reading
    was dropped as one nothing had seen. A third pass would have lifted such a
    read out of a loop that wrote it, and a fourth read one across the loads
    it was widening. All four are answered the same way and in one place: the
    alias analysis gives an element read the place an address of the same
    operands would name, so the pass that asks what stands between two reads
    can be asked about one — and the sharing of two of them is that pass's
    now, where it can see a write between them, rather than the one that
    compares operands and cannot.

    The third was not about globals at all and was found through them. This
    compiler was *nondeterministic*: the phis of a join were placed by
    walking a hash map, so the same source compiled twice gave two different
    programs — and one of them put a loop counter in the register x86-64's
    divide clobbers for itself, and divided by nought. Two bugs, one
    hiding the other: the placement is ordered now, and the divide moves its
    divisor out of the way rather than trusting an allocator that was never
    told the instruction claims two registers. A compiler that answers
    differently on two runs is worse than one that answers wrongly on both,
    since only one of the two can be debugged.

    What a global may be initialised *to* is now exactly what a const may be:
    the same evaluator answers both, so anything it cannot read leaves the
    global starting at nought rather than refusing the program.

    What is still not done is the part that was never only about a segment.
    Nothing checks a constant for range, so a value too big for the type it
    was declared as is neither folded away nor complained about — and a
    division by zero in a const is left unfolded rather than reported, there
    being nothing to report it. A "str" global writes no bytes, an address
    not being known until the linker has run; it wants a relocation into the
    pool, which nothing has asked for yet. And the collector still has to be
    told to look here, there being no other root outside a stack.
  - "gc" is a word on a binding, and what may stand under one is a heap value
    or a pointer (section 2, <var_decl>). Settled as far as the grammar and
    the front end go: it sits between the intro and the name so that it
    annotates the binding, it is orthogonal to "let" and "var", and it buys
    no licence an "unsafe" would otherwise have to give — "unsafe let gc p =
    addr b.p" writes both words because the two say different things about
    the same statement. Lowering turns down what it can see is neither a heap
    value nor a pointer, which is every literal, tuple, closure, range, array
    and reference; what it cannot see — a call, a name, a struct literal, a
    named type — it leaves alone, since the type is the checker's to work
    out and a struct holding what it allocated is exactly where a heap value
    is likely to be. So the rule is enforced in two halves, and only the
    first half exists.
    What a collector *is* is now settled, and it is settled outside the
    language rather than in it: a non-moving mark-and-sweep collector over a
    heap of size-classed spans, marking while the program runs behind a
    hybrid write barrier and sweeping lazily. That is Go's design and it is
    written out in runtime/src/gc.rs. Two things about it reach up into the
    compiler and so belong here. The heap is scanned *precisely*, through a
    descriptor the compiler emits for each type it hands over — one bit per
    word, saying whether that word holds an address — and the roots are
    scanned *conservatively*, because nothing emits a map of what a stack
    frame holds and a word on one is an address or a number with nothing to
    say which. The second is why the collector may not move anything: an
    address that was guessed at can be read and cannot be rewritten.

    What is still not settled is everything about it that is the language's.
    There is no spelling for when it runs, nothing that says whether a "gc"
    binding may be moved out of or handed to a function that took an ordinary
    one, and no account of how it meets the "Drop" and the moving-by-default
    above — a value with a release the compiler places and a value with a
    release the collector places are two answers to one question, and the
    language now has both words and only one of the answers. Nor is it said
    whether "gc" reaches a type ("GC<T>" as a type a signature could take) or
    stays a property of the binding it is written on, which is the difference
    between a word the front end spends and one the whole checker carries —
    and until that is answered a "gc" binding allocates nothing at all. What
    the collector owns today is a closure's captures, a map and a set, and no
    value the word was ever written on.
  - A parameter may take bounds inline and in a <where_clause_opt> at once,
    and the two are joined; a <where_pred> must constrain a parameter the
    signature introduced, so "where i32: Show" is refused (section 2).
    Settled. What a bound *means* is still a question for whatever checks
    traits, and nothing here says it.
  - Where a program begins is settled and what it is handed is not. "main" at
    the top of the root module, taking nothing, its integer answer the exit
    status and everything else exiting zero (section 1). What is missing is
    the way in and the way out. There is no spelling for the arguments a
    process was started with and none for its environment, so the only
    program that can be written is one that computes what it was going to
    compute anyway. And there is no way out of the middle of one: a fn deep
    in a program that has found it cannot go on returns "never" and has
    nothing to call — nothing, that is, but a "%symbol" declaration of
    somebody else's "exit", which is the same answer the language gives for
    every other thing it has not got round to and is not an answer for this
    one. A process that cannot stop when it knows it should is worse off than
    one that cannot read its arguments.

    Nor is it said what "%test" means for any of this. Section 1 says a
    "%test" fn is "collected and run on its own rather than compiled into an
    ordinary build", which is a second entry point described in one clause —
    what collects them, what they are linked against, and whether a suite
    being tested still wants a "main" are all unasked.
